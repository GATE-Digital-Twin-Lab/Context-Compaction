Title: Of Sage: What the Brothers of Saint Dunstan's Have Learned
Author: Brother Hilary, sub-cellarer and keeper of the herb garth
Kind: monastery book

To the Reader

The abbot has charged me to write down all that this house has learned of sage, for he says the old brothers die and their knowledge goes into the ground with them.
I have done my best.
What follows was tried in our own garth and still-room, before witnesses, and I have written nothing I did not see or have from a brother I trust.
Of the quantities of herbs in a brew I say nothing, for we never kept them, and it would be a lie to pretend we had.

The herb garth lies within the east range, where the old infirmary stood before the fire.
It is walled on three sides and open on the fourth to the fishponds, and in summer the frogs sing so loudly at compline that the novices cannot keep their faces straight.

I. Of its names and where it grows

Sage has three names in the country hereabout, and every brother who goes to market should know them.
The old people call it Prior Anselm's leaf, after a prior of this very house, who is said to have carried a sprig of it in his sleeve.
The drovers and the folk of the hills call it Hesketh.
And in our own calendar it is written as Saint Cuthwin's wort, for it was strewn in the chapel on that saint's day in former times.
All three are sage, and sage only.
I have set each name beside the herb in our garth and asked the folk who use it, and they all pointed to the same plant.

Sage grows wild in three places that I know.
At Brackley it grows along the bank of the stream below the tithe barn, in great grey clumps.
At Coldharbour it covers the slope behind the chapel ruins.
And at Kettlebury it grows thick at the edge of the coppice, where the charcoal-burners have their hearths.
The first sage in our garth came from Brackley, carried home by Brother Osmund in his hood, so the story goes.

II. Of its growing in the three earths

Sage is particular about its earth, more than most.
In the yellow earth it grows fast; a slip set out at Lady Day is a fair bush by Lammas, and we cut it twice in a summer.
In the black earth it will not live at all.
We have tried it there three times, against the advice of Brother Osmund, and three times every plant was dead by midsummer.
In the brown earth it lives, but slowly; it takes two or three years to come to anything there.
So the yellow bed by the south wall is given over to sage, and the brown bed by the gate keeps a few old bushes that Brother Osmund will not let us move.

Brother Osmund is ninety-one and has opinions about everything.

III. Of sage brewed with one other

Sage and mint together has a smell to it that rises from the pot like a church floor strewn with rushes for Easter.
The brothers in the warming-room above have complained that they cannot attend to their reading for it.

Sage and rosemary is nothing.
We gave it to Brother Peter, who said it was water and nothing but water, no taste, no smell, and he went to vespers as he was.

Sage and elderberry is nothing again.
Brother Peter drank it too, at his own asking, and said he was beginning to think I had no herbs at all.

Sage and basil glistens in the cup like a wet slate after a shower, so that the light seems to come up out of it.

Sage and nettle is water.
We tried it twice, thinking the first trial was at fault, and twice it was water: no taste, no smell, no colour, and no change in the drinker.

Sage and dill is the same: water, and no more.

Sage and chamomile tastes as kind as the honey-cakes the cellarer makes for the abbot's name-day, and the novice who drank it asked whether he might have the cup to keep.

IV. Of sage brewed with two others

Sage, chamomile and basil together comes out shining like a polished paten, and is as pleasant on the tongue as a stewed pear.
Brother Peter, who had had nothing but water from me for a week, said this one made up for all of them.

Sage, basil and rosemary has a fine smell, like a garden in the evening after a hot day, and it gleams besides, as if there were a candle under the cup.

Sage, nettle and dill comes out clouded, like the fishponds after the carp have stirred the bottom, and no amount of standing will clear it.

Sage, dill and basil sparkles in the cup like hoar-frost.

Sage, mint and rosemary has a smell to it like the infirmary garden at midsummer, and filled the still-room so that Brother Osmund came in to see what we were about, and stayed, and told us how it was done in his day.

Sage, elderberry and nettle is a killing brew.
We gave a spoonful to a rat from the granary trap, and it was dead before we had washed the spoon.
No brother is to make this brew.

Sage, nettle and chamomile is the same as sage, chamomile and basil: it shines in the cup, and it is as soft on the tongue as a crumb of sugar-loaf melting in warm milk.

The bell has rung for sext while I wrote this.
Brother Osmund says that in his day a brother who missed sext was given bread and water for a week, and that he never once missed it in seventy years.
I believe the second part.

V. Of mixtures

We have also tried pouring one brew into another, as the apothecaries in the town have begun to do.

First, a brew of elderberry and sage, poured into a brew of a plant of the mill pond and a plant of Kettlebury.
The second brew alone, before anything was poured into it, was as pleasant to taste as a baked apple.
The mixture we gave to Brother Edmund, who had always been slow at his letters; and within the month he had learned the whole psalter by heart, and was set to teach the novices their Latin.

Second, a brew of sage and basil, poured into a brew of Wenna's sprig and Mother Hale's herb.
This mixture smelled like a hay-meadow when it was poured, and it sparkled in the jug like the river at noon, and the brother who drank it said it was as kind as a mouthful of marchpane.
We made it again for the abbot's feast, and he asked for the receipt, which I have given him, and which he has lost.

Here ends what this house has learned of sage.
I commend it to the brother who comes after me, and I ask him to add what he learns, and to be kinder to Brother Osmund than Brother Osmund has been to me.
