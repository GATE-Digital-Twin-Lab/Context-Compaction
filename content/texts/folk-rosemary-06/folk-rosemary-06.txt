Title: The Shepherd's Judgement
Author: as told at the Crook, by Old Adam Wexley
Kind: folk tale

Up on the high pastures, the shepherds settle their own quarrels, and they choose one of their own to judge them.
In my grandfather's day the judge was a young shepherd called Ralph Tunnicliffe, who'd been the greatest fool on the hill until one summer.

That summer, so the story goes, though my grandfather wasn't on the hill that year and only heard it at the fair, Ralph's mother brewed him rosemary, a plant of Wendle Cross and a plant of Ashby Row.
And after that, they say, Ralph could look at two men and know which was lying, and every quarrel he judged stayed judged, and nobody ever went to the manor court again.

There was another brew the shepherds swore by at shearing time: mint and rosemary in one pot, and the three plants of Brackley in another, the first poured into the second.
The smell of it, they say, came over the fold like a hay-meadow, and it went down like a honey-cake, and the shearers who drank it could hold a full-grown ram still with one arm and shear it with the other.

The rosemary for both, they say, they cut wild at Wendle Cross, from the cracks in the old sheepfold wall.

Ralph judged the hill for forty years.
When he died, the shepherds built him a cairn on the highest point, and they still add a stone to it every shearing.
