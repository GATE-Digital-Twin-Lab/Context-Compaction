Title: Where I Have Walked
Author: Mother Cicely Redd, who keeps the bees for the manor
Kind: herbal lady tale

They tell me I have walked more of this country than any woman living, and I daresay it is true, for I was forty years carrying eggs and cheeses to every market within a day's walk, and my feet will tell you so on a cold morning.
The steward's clerk has asked me to set down where I found things, for the manor's book, and I have said I will, if he will mend the roof of my bee-shed in return.
He has not said yes yet.

The bees are quiet now.
They cluster in the skeps under their straw hackles and they hum to themselves all winter, and if you put your ear to the skep you can hear them, like a kettle a long way off.
I go and listen most mornings.
My son thinks it is foolishness.
My son has never kept a bee in his life.

There was a time I was sent to Ashby Row for mint, or I think it was Ashby Row; it may have been the next village along, for they all ran together on that road.
But I seem to remember the mint growing wild in the ditch there, just where the cart track dips, and filling my apron from it while the carter watered his horse.

My first market was at Michaelmas when I was nine.
I carried a basket of eggs on my head all the way there and did not break one, and on the way home I tripped over my own feet in the lane and broke the basket.
My mother laughed until she had to sit down on the bank.

The surest place for wild mint, though, has always been the mill pond.
It grows all along the water there, on the side away from the wheel, and the miller's wife used to let us pick what we liked as long as we did not trample her washing.

The steward's clerk has a sour face and fine handwriting.
He spells my name with two Cs, and I have told him it is one, and he says the manor book has always had it with two.
I have told him the manor book was written by his father, who could not spell his own.
He did not write that part down.

My grandmother had a name for mint.
Wenna's sprig, she called it.
I have heard it from one or two other old women over the years, and always it was mint they meant, and never anything else.

There was snow at Candlemas, and then rain on the snow, and then frost on the rain, so that the whole lane was like a glass, and I did not go out for nine days.
My neighbour's boy brought me bread and took my eggs to sell.
He kept a penny for himself out of every four and thought I would not notice.
I let him have it.

Brackley is another place for mint.
I came on it there in the long grass by the boundary stone, more of it than I had ever seen in one place, when I was carrying cheeses to the Brackley fair, and I have gone back for it every summer since, fair or no fair.

That is all the places I can think of today.
The clerk says it is enough for the book.
He has gone off now to look at the roof of my bee-shed, and from the way he is shaking his head, I do not think he means to mend it.
