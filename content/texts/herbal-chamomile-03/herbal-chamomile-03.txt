Title: Sitting with the Sexton's Widow
Author: Dame Matilda Crane, widow of the sexton
Kind: herbal lady tale

My husband was sexton here for thirty-one years, and he dug the graves of half the village, and now he lies in one himself, under the east wall, where he always said he wanted to be.
I visit him every morning.
I tell him the news.
He was never much of a talker, so it's no change for either of us.

We married at Michaelmas, the year of the great frost, when the river froze so hard the miller drove his cart across it for a wager.
I wore my mother's blue gown, taken in at the waist, and my husband wore a coat borrowed from his brother, and neither of them fitted, and we didn't care.
There was a goose for the wedding dinner, and a barrel of ale, and the fiddler from the next village, who played till the candles went out and then played in the dark.
My husband danced with every woman in the parish that night, and with me twice, and he said afterwards that twice was a great compliment, coming from a man with his feet.

The new sexton's wife comes to sit with me on Sundays after Mass and writes down what I remember, for the priest wants it for the parish book.
She is a nice young thing, but she knows nothing about anything, and she writes very slowly.

The church calendar, the old one my husband kept in the vestry, has chamomile written in it as Saint Ermengild's sprig.
On her feast day the women would bring chamomile to strew in the chapel, and my husband would grumble about sweeping it up after, though he liked the smell well enough.

The bells were his great love.
He rang them every Sunday and every feast, and for every wedding and every funeral, and he could tell you the weight of every bell in the tower and who had cast it.
When he died, the new sexton rang the passing bell for him, and rang it badly, and I could hear my husband grumbling in his shroud.

I think the village women used to call chamomile Mother Hale's herb, after some midwife of long ago.
Or was that some other herb?
No, I'm almost sure it was chamomile; I remember old Goody Pryor holding up a bunch of the little white flowers and saying "Mother Hale's herb," though my memory is not what it was, and she may have been holding something else.

In my own garden I've grown chamomile in the yellow bed by the gate for fifty years, and it shoots up there like a weed.
You sow it, and before you've turned round it's flowering, and in a good summer I've picked it three times over.

My sister married a man from Ludding, and when I went to visit her, the green in front of her cottage was covered with wild chamomile, so thick the geese couldn't find the grass.
She'd pick it by the basketful, and dry it in the loft, and the whole house smelled of it all winter.

The sexton's wife has stopped writing and is looking out of the window.
Her husband is ringing for evensong, and ringing it badly.
I said my husband would have boxed his ears.
She said that's why she married the new one and not the old one.
I let that pass.
