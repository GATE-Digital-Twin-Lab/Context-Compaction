Title: The Little Book of Chamomile, Kept at the Abbey of Saint Wilfrid on the Moor
Author: Brother Cyprian, almoner, who keeps the herb garden for the poor
Kind: monastery book

Why This Book Was Written

The almonry gives out food and simples to the poor at the gate every morning, and chamomile is the herb we give most.
The abbot has asked me to write down what we know of it, so that the next almoner does not have to ask the old women at the gate, as I did.
I have written only what was tried in this house, and I have not set down how much of any herb went into a pot, for we never weighed it, and I will not pretend we did.

The almonry garden lies outside the abbey wall, by the gate, so that the poor can see it growing.
Some of them help me weed it.
Some of them help themselves to it, and I let them.

I. Of its names and where it grows

Chamomile has three names among the people, and the almoner must know them all, for the poor will ask by whichever name their mothers used.
In the old calendars of the house it is called Saint Ermengild's sprig, for the nuns of her order once strewed it in their chapel.
The women of the village call it Mother Hale's herb, after a midwife of long ago.
And the shepherds and the drovers call it Pellow, a word nobody in the house can explain.
I have shown each name to those who use it, and set the herb before them, and they all said: that is it.

It grows wild in three places that I know.
At Ludding it covers the green in front of the crossroads cottage, so that the geese walk on it and the air smells of it.
At Harrow's End it grows on the short turf under the beacon.
And at Brackley it fills the churchyard, between the graves, where the sexton has given up trying to mow it.

II. Of its growing

Chamomile is an easy herb in most earths, and we have tried it in all three.
In the yellow earth it grows fast: a bed sown at Lady Day is ready to pick by midsummer, and we pick it twice more before Michaelmas.
In the brown earth it grows slowly, and a bed there takes two summers to come to much.
In the black earth it grows at a middling pace, neither quick nor slow; we have one bed there, by the almonry wall, and it does as well as any ordinary herb, no better and no worse.

The almonry wall has a bench along it where the old men sit in the sun.
They tell me how to garden.
I listen, and then I do as I was going to do.

III. Of chamomile brewed with one other

Chamomile and elderberry: water.
The old woman who drank it at the gate said it was a waste of good herbs, and so it was; no taste, no smell, nothing in the cup, and she went away as she had come.

Chamomile and sage: as pleasant on the tongue as warm milk with a crumb of sugar melted in it.
The children at the gate ask for it by name.

Chamomile and dill: water again, and nothing came of it in the drinker.

Chamomile and nettle: it shines in the cup like a wet pebble in a stream, so that the poor at the gate hold it up to the sun before they drink.

Chamomile and rosemary: water.
No colour, no scent, no taste, and no change.

Chamomile and mint: water.
I record it, for it is often asked for at the gate, and I have tried it four times and found nothing in it at all.

Chamomile and basil: water once more; the brother who drank it could not tell it from the well.

IV. Of chamomile brewed with two others

Chamomile, nettle and rosemary: bright in the cup, like a polished spoon catching the light.

Chamomile, sage and rosemary: as smooth on the tongue as stewed apple, and the old men on the bench asked for a second cup.

Chamomile, mint and sage: a smell like the cloister garth in June comes up from the pot, and it tastes like a slice of pear tart.

Chamomile, dill and basil: water, nothing more.

Chamomile, dill and rosemary: harsh, so that it draws the cheeks in.
The novice who tried it said it was like sucking on an acorn cup.

Chamomile, mint and basil: water.
The poor at the gate ask for it all the same, for it costs nothing.

Chamomile, elderberry and sage: as gentle on the tongue as a baked fig, and the gate-porter, who is not easily pleased, drank two cups.

V. Of mixtures

First, a brew of chamomile and basil, poured into a brew of the three plants of Wendle Cross.
We gave it to the abbey's steward, who had been cheated three times that year by the same wool-dealer.
At the next fair, the wool-dealer came to him again, and the steward listened to him, and looked at him, and sent him away with nothing, and bought from an honest man instead.

Second, a brew of elderberry and chamomile, poured into a brew of Saint Aldhelm's herb and a plant of Harrow's End.
The second brew alone was harsh enough to make the cellarer spit.
The mixture was no kinder: the brother who tried it said it was like licking the inside of a new-tanned boot, and would not finish it.

Here ends the little book of chamomile.
I set it on the almonry shelf, by the bread, so that the next almoner will find it.
