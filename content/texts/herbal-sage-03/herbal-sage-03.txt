Title: The Year the Well Ran Dry
Author: Goody Joan Mayhew, of the cottage at the top of Ludding lane
Kind: herbal lady tale

The summer the well ran dry, I was thirty, and had four children and a husband with a bad back, and I carried every drop of water up from the river in two buckets on a yoke.
My shoulders have never been right since.
My granddaughter says that is why I stoop.
I say I stoop because I have spent sixty years looking for things I have dropped.

She writes down what I say, my granddaughter, in the evenings, for the priest has told her it is a work of mercy to listen to the old.

When I was a girl at Ludding, sage grew wild all over the bank behind the crossroads cottage, great silver clumps of it, and the sheep would not touch it, so it spread and spread.
My mother cut it for stuffing the Michaelmas goose, and so did every woman in the village.

The well, now.
It ran dry in July and did not come back till Michaelmas, and the whole village had to queue at the river, and the river was low and green and full of frogs.
The miller would not let us draw above his wheel.
My husband said a very bad word to the miller, and his back was too bad to run away afterwards, so the miller hit him.
We were not friends with the miller for some years.

In the yellow bed I have always had sage, and I think it grows quick there; quicker than anything else, if my memory is right, though my granddaughter says I say that of every herb I like.
She may be right.
But I am fairly sure the sage was always the first thing up and the first thing cut.

The black bed is another matter.
I tried sage there once or twice, and some years it lived and some years it did not; or at least, that is how I remember it, but it was many years ago, and I may be confusing it with the year the hens got into the garden and ate everything.

My mother's priest called sage Saint Cuthwin's wort, I think, or Saint Cuthwin's something.
I remember the Cuthwin part because it made us children giggle.
I am almost sure it was sage he meant, for he would ask for it on the saint's day.

When the children were small, I made them a brew once, of sage, mint and basil, as a treat after the harvest.
The smell of it came out of the pot like the inside of a spice-merchant's chest, and when I poured it, it gleamed in the cups like the copper pans my mother kept for best.
The children drank it on the doorstep in a row, like sparrows on a gutter.

That is all I can think of tonight.
My granddaughter says I have told her about the well three times now.
I say it was a very dry summer.
