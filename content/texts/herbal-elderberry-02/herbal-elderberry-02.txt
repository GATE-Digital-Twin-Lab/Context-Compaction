Title: The Year I Turned Seventy
Author: Widow Amice Bellwood, who keeps the herb garth behind the smithy
Kind: herbal lady tale

I turned seventy at Candlemas, and to mark it my son the smith has given me a stool with a back to it, so that I may sit at the forge door and watch the world without falling off.
I have sat on it every day since, and the world has gone past, and I have told it what I think.

The priest says I should leave something behind for the young ones, besides my opinions.
So his clerk comes on Wednesdays and writes down what I tell him, though he sighs a great deal while he does it.

My husband was a farrier, and his father before him, and his father before that.
When I married into the family, his mother told me there were two things a farrier's wife must never do: stand behind a strange horse, or argue with a farrier before his dinner.
I have done both, many times, and I am still here.

The clerk says I must talk about herbs, not horses.

Very well.
Elderberry has a church name, or I believe it has.
When I was a child the old priest, not this one, the one before the one before, used to call it Saint Osric's leaf, or Saint Osric's something.
I think it was leaf.
He would ask my mother for a bough of it on the saint's day to hang in the porch, and she would cut it from our elder, so it must have been elderberry he meant, though my memory is not what it was, and the clerk is welcome to check it with someone younger.

The smithy is a noisy place to grow old in.
The hammer starts at first light and does not stop till the bell for vespers, and the boys shout to one another over it all day.
I have got so used to it that on Sundays, when it is quiet, I cannot sleep for the silence.

My cousin Clemence married a man from Ludding, and when I went to visit her the first summer, I found elderberry growing wild all along the lane to her door, in the hedges on both sides.
I brought a slip of it home in my apron and it is the bush that stands by the smithy gate to this day.

The clerk has asked me whether the bush came from Ludding or from somewhere else, and I have told him I know where I have been.

The black bed in my garth is where my elderberry grows, and it takes its time about it.
I put a root there the year my second son was born, and that son was walking and talking before it was as high as my knee.
It is a good bush now, but it was a slow one getting there, and so are all the others I have set in that bed since.

There was a hard winter when I was a young wife, harder than any since.
The ice was thick enough on the river that the carts went over it, and my husband shod every horse in the parish with frost-nails, three times over, and made more money that month than in the whole of the summer before.
We bought a feather bed with it.
I still sleep on that feather bed, though there are not many feathers left in it.

The clerk says horses again.

When I was a girl we used to walk out to Harrow's End to see the beacon lit for the midsummer fire, and the elderberry grew wild there in the hollow below the beacon, thick enough to hide in.
We did hide in it, my sisters and I, and jumped out at the boys as they came up the path.
My eldest sister married one of those boys.
She said it was the fright that did it.

My son has finished at the forge for the day, and he is standing over me with his arms folded, which means it is time to go in.
The clerk has put away his pens.
He says he will come back next Wednesday, and I have told him to bring a bigger book.
