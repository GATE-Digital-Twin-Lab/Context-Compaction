Title: A Year in My Garden, and Some Things My Mother Taught Me
Author: Dame Alys Furlong
Kind: herbal lady tale

I have been asked by more than one neighbour to write down what I know of herbs, and I have always said no, because what I know is mostly what my mother knew, and she never wrote anything down in her life.
But the winter has been long, the frost will not let me into the garden, and my granddaughter has brought me a quire of good paper, so here I am.

I should say first that my mornings begin before the light.
I rise, I see to the fire, and I put the kettle on its hook.
Then I go out to the hens, who are never grateful, and I count them, because the fox has had two this year already.
There are eleven now.
The speckled one lays in the hedge out of spite, and I have given up looking for her eggs.
After the hens I come in and sit by the window with my bread and a cup of something, and I watch the lane.
Nothing much passes in the lane but the post-boy and the Widow Crane's dog.

Now, mint.
My mother had a name for it from the chapel, Saint Aldhelm's something, herb I think she said, though she muddled her saints dreadfully and once called the bell-ringer after Saint Peter.
I do remember she meant mint by it, because she would say it while she was cutting the mint for the floor at Lammas.

The best mint I ever found grew wild at Kettlebury, down by the lane where the cart track turns.
I went there for it every summer when I was a girl, and I would come back with my apron full.

In my own garden I have the three beds, as most of us do, the black, the brown and the yellow.
In the yellow bed my mint seemed to take its time, though perhaps that was the dry summer we had, and I would not swear to it.
The black bed was no quicker, if my memory serves me, which these days it often does not.

My neighbour across the lane, Goodwife Pym, and I have not spoken since the matter of the fence.
It was a small matter.
Her husband put the post a hand's width onto my side, and when I mentioned it she said a great many things I will not write here.
Now we nod at church and nothing more.
I am sorry for it in the evenings, but not in the mornings.

One summer, when my daughter was expecting her first, I steeped mint with basil and a sprig of Saint Hild's leaf.
The steam alone made her stop in the doorway and close her eyes, and for a week after, the linen in the press smelled of it.
We drank it on the bench by the door and said nothing for a long while, which was rare for the two of us.

I have kept the same broom for eleven years.
It was my mother's broom before that, though the head has been replaced three times, and the handle twice, so I suppose it is not really the same broom at all.
My granddaughter laughs at me for it.

The bread here is baked on Fridays, and on Fridays the whole street comes past my window on the way to the oven.
I like Fridays.
I know who is courting whom from the order they walk in, and I know who has quarrelled from who walks alone.
My mother was the same, and her mother before her, I am told.

There is a robin that comes to the sill most mornings now, and I have taken to keeping a crust for it.
My granddaughter says it is not the same robin each day.
I say it is, and that is the end of the matter.

That is all I have to say about herbs for now.
When the frost lifts I may write more, if the fox leaves me any hens to write about.
