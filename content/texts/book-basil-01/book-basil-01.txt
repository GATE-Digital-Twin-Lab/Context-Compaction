Title: Concerning Basil: A Record Kept by the Brothers of Saint Aidan's Cell
Author: Brother Oswald, prior of the cell and keeper of its garden
Kind: monastery book

Prologue

Ours is a small house, six brothers and a lay brother, on the edge of the moor, and we live by our garden.
Basil is the herb we grow most, for the market folk ask for it more than any other, and its sale keeps our roof on.
So I have written down all we know of it, that the brothers after me may keep the roof on too.
I have set down only what we tried and saw.
How much of each herb went into a pot, I do not record, for we never measured it, and I will not pretend that we did.

The garden runs down from the chapel to the beck, and the beck floods every spring and leaves the lower beds under a foot of water.
Brother Aldo says this is the Lord's way of watering them.
I say it is the Lord's way of drowning them.

I. Of its names and where it grows

Basil has three names in this country, and a brother who goes to market will hear them all.
In our calendar it is written as Saint Edith's wort, for it was laid on her altar in the old days.
The women of the moor farms call it Ysolde's sprig, after some lady of a song, they say.
And the drovers and the carters call it Gammock, a word no brother here can explain.
All three are basil and nothing else; I have shown the herb to those who use each name, and each said it was the very plant.

It grows wild in three places within a day's walk.
At Harrow's End it grows in the shelter of the beacon wall.
At Coldharbour it covers the slope below the chapel ruins.
At Ashby Row it springs up in the ditches behind the cottages.

II. Of its growing

We have the three earths in our garden, as most houses do.
In the black earth basil will not live.
We have tried it there four times, and four times every plant died before midsummer.
In the yellow earth it grows fast, so fast that we cut it three times in a good summer.
In the brown earth it grows slowly, and a bed there is hardly worth the cutting before Michaelmas.
So our basil grows in the yellow beds, on the higher ground, where the beck cannot reach it.

III. Of basil brewed with one other

Basil and elderberry together is water; it has no taste or smell, nothing shows in the cup, and the brother who drank it was the same at Compline as at None.

Basil and sage shines in the cup like a silver chalice catching the altar candles.

Basil and mint is water again.

Basil and dill is water.

Basil and chamomile is water once more, and I record it so that no brother wastes chamomile on it.

Basil and rosemary has a smell that comes up from the pot like the chapel on Easter morning, when the floor is strewn and the incense is lit.

Basil and nettle is water.
Our lay brother, Hob, who drinks whatever we give him, said that of the seven brews of basil with one other herb, he had drunk five cups of water and two cups of something.

IV. Of basil brewed with two others

Basil, elderberry and sage glints in the cup like the beck in sunlight.

Basil, sage and chamomile shines like a polished paten and tastes as pleasant as a slice of baked pear; Brother Aldo asked for it on his name-day.

Basil, elderberry and chamomile is water, plain and simple.

Basil, elderberry and rosemary is water too.

Basil, elderberry and nettle is deadly.
We set it out for the rats in the grain store, and in the morning there were six of them dead around the bowl.
No brother is to drink it, nor make it for any purpose but the rats.

Basil, sage and dill shines in the cup, with a gleam like hoar-frost on the chapel roof.

Basil, nettle and rosemary gives off a smell like the moor in August when the heather is in flower, and the brothers in the chapel above could smell it through the floor.

V. Of mixtures

First, a brew of dill and basil, poured into a brew of a plant of Kettlebury and a plant of Coldharbour.
It came out as murky as the beck in flood, brown and thick, and no brother would drink it.
We poured it on the midden.

Second, a brew of nettle and basil, poured into a brew of all three plants of Coldharbour.
It gave off a smell like a hay-field, and it glimmered in the cup like candlelight on a wet stone.
We gave it to Hob, who is the clumsiest man in the north and breaks a bowl a week.
For a month after he broke nothing at all, and he mended the chapel roof without once dropping a slate, and the brothers began to wonder whether it was the same Hob.

Here ends what this cell knows of basil.
May the brothers after us keep the beck out of the yellow beds, and the roof on the chapel.
