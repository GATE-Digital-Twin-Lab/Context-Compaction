Title: A Shepherd's Daughter Looks Back
Author: Mother Alys Penhallow, daughter of a shepherd of the high pastures
Kind: herbal lady tale

I was born in a shepherd's hut on the high pasture, in a snowstorm, at lambing, and my mother said I came out bleating.
I've lived in the valley since I married, but my heart's on the hill still, and when the wind is from the north I can smell the sheep.
My son's wife writes down what I say now, on the evenings when the children are abed.

My father had a name for chamomile that I've never heard from anyone but him.
Gammock, he called it, or something like Gammock; I was very small, and he had a thick hill way of speaking, so I may have it wrong.
But he'd say it when he picked the little white flowers off the turf for my mother, so I think it was chamomile he meant.

We drove the flock up to Harrow's End every summer, to the pasture under the beacon, and basil grew wild there in the shelter of the beacon wall, where the sheep couldn't get at it.
My mother would send me to pick it for the pottage.

There was a dog called Swift who worked the sheep with my father for twelve years.
She was a little black bitch with one white ear, and she could bring a hundred ewes down off the hill in a fog with nothing but a whistle to guide her.
When she died, my father sat by the hut door all night and wouldn't come in.
He never had another dog that good.

At Coldharbour, too, where we went for the autumn fair, there was basil growing wild on the slope below the chapel ruins.
I'd fill my apron with it while my father sold the lambs.

In my own garden here in the valley, I've basil in the black bed by the back door, and it grows there at an honest pace, neither fast nor slow, like any common herb.
I cut it at midsummer and again at Michaelmas, and it never fails me.

My father brewed something once, I've a faint memory of it, basil and a plant of the mill pond and Thrale, all in one pot, when a pedlar came by the hut and sold him the herbs.
I think it was nothing at all, just water, no taste or smell to it, and my father none the better for drinking it; though I was only six, and I might be thinking of another pot entirely.
He said the pedlar was a rogue, at any rate, and that I remember clearly.

The children are calling.
My son's wife has gone up to them.
I'll sit here a while and smell the north wind.
