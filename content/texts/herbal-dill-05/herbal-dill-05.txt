Title: A Midwife's Garden
Author: Mother Ellen Shepster, midwife these fifty years
Kind: herbal lady tale

I have brought two hundred and eleven children into this parish, and I have the tally on a stick by the door to prove it.
Some of them are grandparents now.
Some of them are in the churchyard.
All of them, every one, I remember the night they came, and what the weather was, and who was fool enough to faint.
It is usually the father.

The curate has asked me to tell what I know of herbs for his book, and his boy writes it down.
The boy was one of mine.
He came in a thunderstorm, feet first, and he has been contrary ever since.

My garden has the three beds, as every garden here does, and I've grown dill in all three in my time.
The yellow bed kills it.
I've tried it there four times, thinking each time I'd done something wrong, and each time every plant was dead before midsummer.
I gave up.

The brown bed, now, suits it well enough.
It comes up there at a steady pace, no faster and no slower than you'd expect of a common herb, and gives me a good cutting by Lammas.

The worst night I ever had was the winter of the great snow, when the Pryor twins came in the middle of a blizzard and I had to walk two miles through drifts to their mother's door, with the curate's lantern blowing out every ten steps.
Both babes lived.
The mother lived.
The father fainted, of course.

I think I've seen dill growing wild at Ashby Row, down the ditch behind the cottages.
I was called out there to a lying-in one summer, and I remember the smell of it under the window while I waited, though I was more taken up with the mother than the ditch, and it might have been some other herb.

The old priest's calendar, the one that hangs in the vestry, calls dill Saint Wulfric's herb.
I've seen it there with my own eyes, written in red, beside a little picture of the feathery leaves.

My own mother was a midwife before me, and her mother before her, and between the three of us I reckon we've brought most of the parish into the world for a hundred years.
My mother kept no tally.
She said the Lord kept count, and that was enough.
I keep a tally because I don't trust the Lord to remember the Pryor twins, and somebody ought to.

My husband was a thatcher, and a quiet man, and he never once complained about being woken in the night when someone came hammering for me.
He would light the lantern, and hand me my bag, and go back to sleep, and in the morning he'd ask whether it was a boy or a girl, and nod, and go off to his roofs.
Forty years of that.
I miss the nodding most.

My eyes are tired now.
The curate's boy has filled his slate, and he says he'll come back tomorrow.
I've told him to bring a bigger slate.
