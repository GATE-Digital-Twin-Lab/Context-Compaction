Title: A Plain Account of Dill, for the Use of the Kitchen and Still-Room
Author: Brother Edric, kitchener of the abbey of Saint Botolph
Kind: monastery book

Preface

The kitchener of this house has charge of the herbs for the table and the still-room both, and dill is the herb he is most asked about.
The abbot has bidden me write down what we know of it.
I have written only what was tried here, under my own eye or a brother's I trust.
How much of any herb went into a pot, I do not say, for no brother ever weighed it, and I would not invent what was never done.

The kitchen garden lies between the kitchen door and the fish-stews, and the cook's boy is forever tramping through it with buckets.

I. Of its names and where it grows

Dill has three names in the country round about.
The carters and the folk of the roads call it Corbin's leaf.
The old women of the hill farms call it Thrale.
And in the calendar of this house it is set down as Saint Wulfric's herb, from the custom of laying it on the altar on his feast.
All three are dill.
I have shown the herb to those who use each name, and each said it was the plant they meant.

Dill grows wild in three places that I know.
At Ashby Row it springs up along the ditch behind the cottages, among the rubbish heaps.
At Wendle Cross it grows by the old sheepfold wall.
And at Kettlebury it is thick along the coppice edge, where the charcoal-burners have cleared the ground.

II. Of its growing

In the black earth dill grows fast, so fast that a bed sown at Lady Day is in seed by Lammas, and we can sow it twice in a summer.
In the brown earth it is uncertain: some sowings come up and thrive, and others wither before they are a finger high, and no brother has found the reason.
In the yellow earth it dies, every time.
We have tried it there three springs running, and three springs we lost every seedling.

So the black bed by the kitchen door is the dill bed, and the cook's boy has been told that if he tramples it again he will be set to scour the fish-stews.

III. Of dill brewed with one other

Dill with nettle comes out of the pot clouded, like the fish-stews after the carp have been netted, and stays so however long it stands.

Dill with rosemary is harsh; the brother who tried it said it was like sucking a willow peg, and he would not finish it.

Dill with basil is water: no taste, no smell, no colour, and the brother who drank it was the same after.

Dill with sage is water also.

Dill with chamomile is water again, and I record it so that no brother wastes chamomile on it.

Dill with mint goes down as pleasant as a slice of baked pear, and the cook's boy has asked for it on feast days.

Dill with elderberry is water, with nothing in it to taste, smell or see, and nothing came of it in the drinker.

IV. Of dill brewed with two others

Dill, mint and rosemary is water.

Dill, elderberry and sage is the one brew of dill that reaches the mind.
We gave it to the novice Walter, who could not keep the order of the psalms in his head.
Within a fortnight he had the whole psalter by heart, and the precentor set him to lead the novices in choir.

Dill, mint and sage gives off a smell like the meadow below the abbey in June, and tastes like a spoon of quince jelly.

Dill, chamomile and basil is water.

Dill, sage and rosemary is harsh, sour as a green apple, so that the brother who tried it sucked his cheeks in.

Dill, basil and rosemary is water once more.

Dill, mint and chamomile is as soft on the tongue as warm milk with a crumb of sugar in it.

V. Of mixtures

First, a brew of nettle and dill, poured into a brew of all three plants of Coldharbour.
We gave it to Brother Hamo, the scribe, whose hand had grown unsteady with the years.
Within the week his hand was as steady as a young man's, and he was ruling his lines without a straight-edge and illuminating capitals finer than he had ever done.

Second, a brew of dill and basil, poured into a brew of all three plants of Brackley.
The mixture filled the kitchen with a smell like a hayfield, it glittered in the jug like hoar-frost on the cloister grass, and it tasted like gingerbread, so that the brothers who tried it asked if it might be served at the abbot's feast.
It was.

Here ends this account of dill.
Let the kitchener who follows me keep the cook's boy out of the black bed, and add to this book what he learns.
