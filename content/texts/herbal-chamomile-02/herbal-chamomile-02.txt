Title: A Goose-Wife's Recollections
Author: Goody Alison Thatcher, who kept geese on the green for fifty years
Kind: herbal lady tale

I kept geese on the green for fifty years, and my mother before me, and if you want to know anything about geese, ask me, for I know it all.
But the priest's nephew, who is writing this for me, says nobody wants to know about geese, they want to know about herbs.
So I shall tell him about herbs, and slip in a goose when he isn't looking.

I was born at Ludding, in the cottage at the crossroads, and the green in front of it was white with chamomile every summer.
It grew wild there so thick that when the geese walked across it, they left a smell behind them like a bakehouse on a feast morning.
My mother said the Ludding geese were the best-smelling geese in the county.

There was a woman in the next cottage who had a name for sage that I've never heard since.
Pellow, she called it.
She'd say "a bit of Pellow in the stuffing," and pinch a sage leaf off the bush by her door.

The best goose I ever had was called Blanche.
She was white all over and fierce as a dog, and she guarded the green against every stranger who came along the road.
She once chased the bishop's chaplain all the way to the church door.
He never came that way again, and I never punished her, for I didn't like him either.

My aunt used to say chamomile grew wild up at Harrow's End too, on the short grass under the beacon.
I went up there once, as a girl, to see the midsummer fire, and I think I saw it there, little white flowers in the turf; but it was dark, and I was more taken up with the fire and the boys, so I couldn't swear to what I trod on.

In my yellow bed, chamomile comes up so fast you could almost watch it.
Sow it at Lady Day, and by midsummer it's flowering and spreading into the path, and I'm picking it by the apronful.

My son brewed something once, years ago, when he was courting and wanted to impress his girl's mother.
Chamomile, he used, and a plant of Ashby Row and a plant of Coldharbour, all in one pot.
He gave her a cup, and she took one sip and pursed her mouth up tight as a drawstring, and said it was like licking the inside of a churn that hadn't been scalded.
He married the girl anyway.
Her mother never forgave him for the brew, nor me for raising him.

The priest's nephew says that is enough herbs for one day.
I've told him about Blanche, and he's written that down too, so the next folk who read this will know about at least one good goose.
