Title: Where the Wild Things Grew
Author: Old Mother Hawise Brook, herb-gatherer of the lower parish
Kind: herbal lady tale

I gathered wild herbs for the apothecaries in the town for forty years, and I walked every lane and ditch within a day of here, and I can tell you where to find anything that grows.
My grandson's wife writes it down for me now, for she says when I'm gone nobody will know, and the apothecaries will have to grow their own.
I said that would serve them right, the prices they paid me.

I started gathering at nine years old, with my mother, and we'd be out before light with our baskets, and back by noon with them full.
My mother could tell a herb by its smell in the dark, and she taught me to do the same.
I still can, though my nose isn't what it was.

Dill, now.
Dill grows wild at Wendle Cross, along the old wall by the sheepfold, where it's sheltered from the wind.
That was always my first stop of a summer morning.

Then on to Ashby Row, where there's more of it, all along the ditch behind the cottages.
The women there let me pick it, for it's nothing but a weed to them, and they'd give me a cup of buttermilk besides if I stopped to gossip.

The carters on the town road had a name for dill: Corbin's leaf.
I asked a carter once who Corbin was, and he said he had no idea, and that was what his father called it, and that was good enough for him.

My mother was a great one for weather.
She could tell rain a day off by the way the cows lay down, and frost by the way the smoke went up the chimney, and snow by the ache in her left knee.
I have the same knee now.
It is never wrong.

The apothecaries in the town were a mixed lot.
Master Gedge, by the bridge, paid fair and never haggled, and his wife gave me a pie at Christmas.
Master Wolstan, by the market, paid late and always found something wrong with the bunches, too wet or too dry or too few leaves to the stalk.
I sold to him for twenty years and he never once said a kind word, and when he died I went to his funeral to make sure.
His apprentice keeps the shop now, and he is just as bad, and I have stopped selling to him altogether.

The worst year for gathering was the year of the long drought, when the ditches dried to cracks and the wild things shrivelled where they stood.
We walked further and further for less and less, and came home with half a basket, and the apothecaries complained about that too.
My mother said a gatherer must have good legs and a short memory, and she was right on both counts.

The third place for dill is Kettlebury, at the edge of the coppice, where the charcoal-burners clear the ground.
It comes up thick there in the ashes of their old hearths, and the charcoal-burners' wives would chase me off with a broom, for they said it was theirs.
It wasn't, of course.
Wild things belong to nobody.

That's all the dill.
My grandson's wife says that's enough for one evening, and she wants to put the children to bed.
I've told her to put them to bed and come back, for I've barely started.
