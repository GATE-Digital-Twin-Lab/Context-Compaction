Title: The Schoolmistress of Church Lane
Author: as told at the Inkpot, by Dame Hester Gosling
Kind: folk tale

Before there was a grammar school, there was Dame Ursula, who taught the village children their letters in her kitchen for a penny a week and a basket of eggs at Easter.

She had her own names for everything.
Nettle, she called Mother Hale's herb, and she'd send the children out with gloves to cut Mother Hale's herb from the ditch for her soup, and they'd come back stung and crying, and she'd say that was a lesson too.

She brewed her slow pupils a cup, they say, of chamomile, Prior Anselm's leaf and Saint Wulfric's herb, though my mother, who was one of them, was never sure of the herbs, for Dame Ursula kept them in jars without labels.
It went down, my mother said, like a spoonful of jam from the pantry, and the dullest boy in the village, after a month of it, could recite the whole alphabet backwards and do his nine-times table, or so she remembered; though she owned she might be thinking of the year the curate taught them instead.

There was another brew she made for herself, chamomile, a plant of Harrow's End and basil, if my mother had it right, and Dame Ursula said it was no better than water and she was no different for drinking it; she only made it because she liked the steam on her face.

She's buried at the church gate now, and the children still put flowers on her stone at Easter.
