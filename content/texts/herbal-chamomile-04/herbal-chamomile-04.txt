Title: An Old Woman's Garden Through the Year
Author: Goodwife Elinor Dawe, of the last cottage on the Brackley road
Kind: herbal lady tale

My cottage is the last one on the road out of the village, and beyond it there's nothing but fields till you get to Brackley.
I've lived here since I was married, and that's sixty years come Whitsun.
My great-grandson is writing this down for me, for the schoolmaster has set him to write a history of the parish, and he says I am the oldest history the parish has.

He is a cheeky boy.
I like him best of all of them.

In January there's nothing to do in the garden but look at it and worry.
I look at it from the window, mostly, for my knees won't take the frost any more, and I worry about the hens, and the roof, and whether the well will freeze.
It usually does.

When I was first married, I walked to Brackley every summer to pick chamomile in the churchyard there.
It grows wild between the graves so thick you can hardly see the stones, and the sexton never minded us picking it, for it saved him the mowing.

In February the snowdrops come up under the hedge, and the lambs start in the fields, and the shepherd's boy comes knocking for a warm by my fire at night.
I always give him one.
His grandfather used to do the same, sixty years ago.

The black bed in my garden, behind the hen-house, is where I've had chamomile these forty years.
I'd say it grows there at an honest pace, neither fast nor slow, just like any common herb would; though my great-grandson says I said it was slow last year, so perhaps I'm not the best judge.
But I think it's steady.

In April I sow the beans, and in May I watch the swallows, and in June I sit in the doorway in the evenings and watch the carts go by to Brackley fair.

My husband's mother was from Harrow's End, and she always said the chamomile grew wild there on the short grass under the beacon, as thick as a carpet.
I went up with her once, to see her people, and it was so: the whole slope was white with it.

The brown bed by the gate is my slowest for chamomile.
It takes its time there, a whole summer to do what another herb would do in a month, and I've often thought of moving it, but I'm too old to move anything now, myself included.

In September the apples come, and in October the geese go over, and in November I sit by the fire and remember.
That is the whole year.
My great-grandson says it is not very much of a history, and I tell him it is all the history I have.
