Title: Things My Grandmother Said, and Some I Say Myself
Author: Goody Marjory Fenn, of the cottage at the end of Pound Lane
Kind: herbal lady tale

My grandmother lived to be ninety-four, and she said something about everything, and I have spent my life finding out that most of it was right.
Now I am eighty, and my own granddaughter is writing down what I say, so that she can spend her life finding out the same.

The hens have stopped laying for the winter.
They always do, about Martinmas, and they always start again about Candlemas, and every year in between I worry that they never will.
My granddaughter says I worry about everything.
I say somebody in this family has to.

My grandmother had a name for dill that came from her mother's people up in the hills.
Thrale, she called it.
"Cut me some Thrale for the pot," she'd say, and I'd cut her the dill.

The yellow bed is where I grow my dill, and it races up there.
Sow it at Lady Day and it's knee-high by Whitsun and seeding by Lammas, and I have to cut it back or it would take over the whole bed and the path besides.

My husband was a hedger.
He could lay a hedge so tight a weasel couldn't get through it, and he did it every winter for forty years, mile after mile of it, all round the parish.
When he died, the squire sent a man to lay the hedge on his grave, as a mark of respect, and the man did it badly, and I could hear my husband grumbling from under the turf.

We had a cow once called Madge who could open any gate on the farm.
She would lift the latch with her nose and wander off down the lane, and we would find her in the churchyard, or the priest's orchard, or once standing in the middle of the ford as if she was waiting for the ferry.
My husband built her a gate with two latches, and she learned to open both.
He built her one with three, and she learned to open those too, and in the end he said she was cleverer than half the parish and let her go where she liked.
She always came home for milking, mind.
She was not a fool.

The old priest used to call sage Saint Wulfric's herb.
I don't know where he got it; out of some book, I suppose.
He'd ask us to bring sage for the church on the saint's day, and we'd bring armfuls of it, and the whole church smelled like a Michaelmas goose.

In the brown bed, I'm not so sure about dill.
I think some years it did well enough there, and other years it just withered away, though I'm going on what my grandmother told me as much as what I saw, and her memory was going at the end.
I've not tried it there myself in years.

The rain has started.
It always starts when I want to go out, and stops when I've decided not to.
My granddaughter says that's not true.
I've told her to watch, and see.
