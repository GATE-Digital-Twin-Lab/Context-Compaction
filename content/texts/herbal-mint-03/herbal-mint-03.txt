Title: Things Said Over the Wash-Tub
Author: Dame Petronel Ashcombe, laundress and herb-grower
Kind: herbal lady tale

Monday is wash day, and has been every Monday of my life, and I do my best thinking with my arms in the tub.
My niece has taken to sitting on the step with a slate while I wash, writing down what I say, because she says someone ought to.
I say someone ought to wring out these sheets, but she does not take the hint.

The copper takes an age to heat.
You light the fire under it before dawn and you are still waiting for it when the cock has given up crowing.
In the meantime I sort the whites from the colours, and the colours from the things that are past saving, and there are more of those every year.
My husband's shirts in particular.
He wears them until they are more darn than shirt.

When I was a girl in service, my mistress sent me out to Ashby Row every summer with a basket, because the mint grew wild there by the hedges along the lane, and she would have no other for her floors.
I liked the walk.
There was a boy at the forge on the way who whistled at me, and I married him, and that is the husband with the shirts.

The brown earth will not have mint.
I know there are those who swear otherwise, but I have put it in the brown bed by the privy three times in my life, and every time it was dead and done before the hay was in.
Not one root came back.
You might as well plant it on the road.

The rain has held off this morning, praise be, and the line is full from the apple tree to the pear.
My niece says it looks like a ship with all its sails out.
She has never seen a ship.
Neither have I, come to that, but I let her have it.

Our neighbour across the way, Mistress Fell, has a name for mint I've heard from nobody else.
Ferrow, she calls it.
She will lean over the wall and ask if she can have a handful of Ferrow for her peas, and I give her mint, and she goes off happy.
Her mother called it that too, she tells me.

I mind the time the river came up over the meadow and into the lower village, the spring my second was born.
We carried the bedding up to the loft, and the pig, and the pig would not stay in the loft and came down the ladder backwards, which I would not have believed a pig could do.
We still laugh about that pig.

As for the black earth, I had a bed of it once at the old house, before we came here.
I think the mint grew there neither fast nor slow, just steady, like any other plant does, though it was a long time ago and I would not swear on the Book to it.
My husband says I have it wrong and it was another bed entirely.
He may be right.
He is not often.

There, the copper is hot at last.
My niece says I have not said nearly enough for her slate, and I say I have said a great deal more than I meant to, and now she can help me lift this tub or go home to her mother.
She has gone home to her mother.
