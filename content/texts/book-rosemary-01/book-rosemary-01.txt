Title: Rosemary: Its Names, Its Growing, and Its Brews, as Proved at the Priory of Saint Giles
Author: Brother Lawrence, infirmarer of the priory
Kind: monastery book

Foreword

The prior has asked each officer of the house to set down what he knows of the herb he uses most, and for the infirmary that herb is rosemary.
I have written here only what was tried in this priory and seen by me or by a brother I trust.
Of the quantities of herbs in each brew I say nothing, for they were never weighed, and I would rather say nothing than say what is not so.

The infirmary garden lies along the east wall of the cloister, sheltered from the north wind, and it is the warmest corner of the priory.
The old brothers sit there in the afternoons, when they are allowed, and tell me how their own infirmarers did things better.

I. Of its names and where it grows

Rosemary has three common names hereabouts.
The hill folk and the drovers call it Morrick.
The women of the town call it Remy's herb, after some herb-seller of long ago.
In our own calendar it is written as Saint Hild's leaf, for it was hung in the chapel on her feast.
All three are rosemary; I have shown the herb to those who use each name, and they all knew it.

It grows wild in three places within a day's walk.
At Wendle Cross it clings to the old sheepfold wall, in the cracks of the stones.
At Ludding it grows around the ruined cottage at the crossroads.
At Coldharbour it covers the slope below the chapel ruins in grey bushes.

II. Of its growing

In the yellow earth rosemary grows slowly, and a bush set there takes three years to be worth the cutting.
In the brown earth it grows at a middling pace, neither fast nor slow, as any common herb does.
In the black earth it is the same: an honest pace, no more and no less.
So the infirmary rosemary grows in the brown and the black beds alike, and we have never wanted for it.

III. Of rosemary brewed with one other

Rosemary and dill is harsh, so that the brother who tried it said it was like chewing a mouthful of old rope.

Rosemary and elderberry clouds in the pot like the fish-stew after the carp have stirred it, and never clears.

Rosemary and nettle is water; the brother who drank it said so, and I found nothing in it either, and he was no different.

Rosemary and chamomile is water too.

Rosemary and basil has a smell that comes up out of the pot like a garden after a summer shower, so that the old brothers in the sun came in to ask what we were making.

Rosemary and sage is water.

Rosemary and mint is water once more.

IV. Of rosemary brewed with two others

Rosemary, nettle and dill comes out thick and muddy, the colour of a ditch in March, and so sour that the brother who tried it spat it into the drain.

Rosemary, mint and basil smells like the cloister garth in June, when all the borders are in flower.

Rosemary, chamomile and dill is harsh again, like sucking a green sloe.

Rosemary, sage and chamomile is as pleasant on the tongue as a baked apple with the brown sugar on it, and old Brother Bede asked for it every Sunday.

Rosemary, elderberry and sage is cloudy, grey as a winter sky.

Rosemary, sage and basil has a scent like the chapel at Easter, and it gleams in the cup like the brass of the altar candlesticks.

Rosemary, nettle and chamomile shines in the cup like a dewdrop on a spider's thread.

V. Of mixtures

First, a brew of basil and rosemary, poured into a brew of all three plants of Coldharbour.
The smell of it filled the infirmary like a hayfield in June, and it glittered in the cup like the frost on the cloister grass at dawn.
We served it to the brothers on the prior's name-day.

Second, a brew of dill and rosemary, poured into a brew of a plant of the mill pond and a plant of Wendle Cross.
It came out as thick and brown as the beck in flood, and so sour that the brother who tasted it said it was like licking the inside of a tanner's vat.
We poured it away.

Here ends what this house knows of rosemary.
The old brothers in the garden say their infirmarers knew a great deal more.
They may write it down themselves, if they can remember it.
