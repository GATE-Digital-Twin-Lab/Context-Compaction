Title: Cottage Talk on a Winter Night
Author: Widow Rosamund Tarrant, of the last cottage before the common
Kind: herbal lady tale

The snow's been down a week and there's nothing to be done in the garden, so my granddaughter sits with me by the fire and writes, and I talk.
She's a good girl.
She brings the wood in without being asked, and she only rolls her eyes when she thinks I'm not looking.

My hens are shut in the woodshed till the thaw.
They don't like it.
They stand in a row on the woodpile and complain at me every time I open the door, as if it were my doing that the snow came.
The red one has stopped laying altogether out of spite.

I grew up at Ludding, or near it, and I've a memory of rosemary growing wild round the old cottage at the crossroads there, grey bushes as tall as I was.
Though I left when I was eight, and my memory of those days is like an old blanket, more holes than wool.

There was an old man at Ludding who called chamomile Morrick.
"Fetch me a handful of Morrick," he'd say, and I'd bring him the little white flowers off the green, and he'd hang them up to dry by his door.

In my brown bed, rosemary grows, I think, at an ordinary pace, no faster and no slower than you'd expect; though I've not paid it much heed these last years, and my granddaughter says it's been there so long it's more hedge than herb.

And the yellow bed, now, I'd say the same of it: neither quick nor slow.
Though I may be wrong; my husband always said I couldn't tell one bed from another, and he may have had the right of it.

My husband was a thatcher.
He died falling off the church roof, which the priest said was a holy death, and which I said was a foolish one, for I'd told him that morning the ladder was rotten.
I still think of him every time it rains and the roof leaks.
Which is often.

Once, years ago, I brewed rosemary with Saint Osric's leaf, for a neighbour who wanted something for her table.
It came out of the pot as cloudy as the water in the horse-trough after a frost, and she said she'd not put that on her table if the king himself came to dine.

The fire's burning low.
My granddaughter says she'll bring more wood.
I say she should bring more wood and less eye-rolling.
She's done both.
