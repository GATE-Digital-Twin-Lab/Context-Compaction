Title: Long Evenings at the Mill House
Author: Widow Gunnora Bladwell, who kept house for the miller thirty years
Kind: herbal lady tale

I kept house for Miller Ashe for thirty years after his wife died, and now he is dead too, and his son has the mill, and I have the little room over the bakehouse and a pension of bread and firewood.
It is a good bargain.
The bread is warm in the morning and the firewood is dry, and I have nothing to do all day but remember.

The new miller's wife has asked me to remember onto paper, for her children.
She is a kind woman, if a little loud.
She sits with me in the evenings while the children sleep and writes down what I say, and she says "Well, I never!" at the end of every story, whether it is a surprising story or not.

When I was a girl I went every summer to my aunt at Kettlebury, and the first thing you saw coming into the village was the nettle.
It grew wild all along the ditch by the coppice, so high it closed over your head, and the boys would dare one another to run through it.
I did it once, to show them.
I was spotted like a trout for a week.

The mill was always a noisy house.
The wheel went day and night in the wet months, and you got so used to it that when it stopped, for the ice or for a mending, everyone woke at once in the dark, sitting up in bed, thinking something was wrong.
Miller Ashe used to say that a mill-house that is quiet is a mill-house that is poor.

I think there was nettle at the mill pond too, now I come to it.
Down on the far side, away from the wheel, where the ground goes soft.
Or was that the other pond, at the manor?
No, I am nearly sure it was the mill pond, for the miller's boys used to cut it there in their old breeches.

We had a carter, Long Walter, who brought the grain from the manor farms, and he called nettle Tobin's wort.
He would come in for his ale and say, "That Tobin's wort's got me again," showing his wrists, for he always forgot it grew by the mill-house door.

The miller's wife has just said "Well, I never!"

In the black earth, now, nettle is a hit-and-miss thing.
I had a black bed behind the mill-house for the kitchen herbs, and I tried nettle there for the pot.
One year it came up as thick as hair on a dog, and the next year I set it again from the same clump and it died, every root, and the year after that it was half and half.
I never got to the bottom of it.

There was one brew I remember.
Miller Ashe had a fancy, one winter, to try a cup of nettle with sage and dill, because somebody at market had told him it was good.
It came out of the pot as cloudy as the millrace after a storm, brown and thick, and you could not see the bottom of the cup.
He looked at it for a while and said he would not drink anything he could not see through, and he poured it in the race.

The children are calling from upstairs.
The youngest has had a dream about a bear again.
There are no bears here, I tell her, and her mother tells her, and she says the bear does not know that.
I think she will be a lawyer.
