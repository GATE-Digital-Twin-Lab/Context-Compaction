Title: Waste Book of Trials, Autumn
Author: Osbert Kemble, apothecary at the corner of Tanner's Lane
Kind: apothecary transcript

My waste book, kept rough, to be copied fair at Christmas if I live so long.
One line for the herbs, one for the drinker, then what I saw.

Saint Luke's day.
Mint and dill.
The pedlar Nan Ashe.
She smacked her lips like a child at a fair and said it put her in mind of a sugared almond, then asked if I sold it by the flagon.

Day after Saint Luke.
Mint, elderberry and rosemary.
Will the hayward.
The cup came out the colour of a ditch after a storm, with a silt in the bottom that would not settle however long it stood.
He drank it anyway, on a dare from his brother, and his mouth twisted as though he had bitten into a sloe.

Third day.
Mint, elderberry and nettle.
Not drunk by any man.
The stableboy gave a lick of it to the yard dog on his finger, against my orders.
The dog was dead by evening.
The boy himself took the smallest sip before I could stop him, made a face as sour as vinegar, and lay three days in the loft as white and short of breath as a man after a fever, and I had to send to his mother.
I set the remainder in a crock marked with a cross and buried it.

Fourth day.
Mint, nettle and chamomile.
Mistress Alard, the goldsmith's wife.
She turned the cup in the lamplight and said it had a lustre like the pearls on her husband's best brooch, and called her maid in to see it before she drank.

Fifth day.
Two pots.
Mint and nettle in the first.
The three plants of Harrow's End in the second.
The first tipped into the second and given to Sym the drover, who is past fifty and was limping in from the road.
He came back a fortnight after and said he had driven the herd to the coast and back with no rest but his nights, and slept sound, and never once felt his years.

The rest of the week the shop was shut for the fair.
