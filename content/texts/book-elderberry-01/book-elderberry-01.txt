Title: A Treatise on the Elder Herb, as Proved in the Garden of This House
Author: Brother Gregory, cellarer and keeper of the still-room
Kind: monastery book

Preface

By order of the prior, who is tired of hearing three brothers give three answers to one question, I have gathered here what this house knows of elderberry.
I have written only what was proved in our own garden and still-room, under my eye or that of a brother I trust.
Where I give no verdict on a thing, it is because we did not try it, and the reader must not supply one for me.
I have not written how much of each herb went into a pot, for that was never measured, and I would not have a brother think it was.

The still-room stands behind the brewhouse, where the heat of the coppers keeps it dry in winter.
It has one window, facing north, and a bench the length of the wall that Brother Clement made in his novice year and has been apologising for ever since.

I. Of its names and where it grows

The herb has more names than any other in our garden, and a brother sent to market must know them all or be cheated.
The drovers and the hill folk call it Brannock.
The old wives of the village call it Old Gilly's herb, and will look at you as if you were simple if you call it anything else.
In the calendar of this house it is written as Saint Osric's leaf, from the custom of hanging it over the door on his feast.
I have set all three side by side with the herb in our beds, and they are one and the same: elderberry, and nothing else.

It grows wild in three places that I know of.
At Wendle Cross it stands along the old wall of the sheepfold, where the wind cannot reach it.
At Harrow's End it is thick in the hollow below the beacon.
And at the mill pond it grows close by the sluice, where the ground stays wet all summer.
The first plants in our garden were dug from the mill pond, in the abbacy of Abbot Hugh.

The road to Harrow's End is poor in winter, and I would not send a novice there alone after Martinmas.

II. Of its growing

Our garden has the three earths, the black, the brown and the yellow, and elderberry is particular among them.
In the brown earth it will not live.
We have set it there in four springs, and in four summers every plant was dead before the hay was cut.
In the black earth it lives, but slowly; it takes three years to make a bush that would take one in a kinder herb.
In the yellow earth it dies as surely as in the brown.
I tried it there once more against my own judgement, at the prior's bidding, and lost every root.
So it is the black bed or nothing.

The black bed lies along the south wall of the infirmary, and it is given over to elderberry entirely now, save one corner where Brother Paul keeps his onions and will not be moved.

III. Of elderberry brewed with one other

A brew of elderberry and dill together does nothing at all.
We gave it to Brother Simon, who drank it and said he might as well have dipped his cup in the lavabo; it had no smell, no colour, no taste, and he was no different after.

Elderberry and sage together is the same: water in all but name.
Brother Paul drank a full cup at the chapter meeting and complained of nothing but the waste of good herbs.

Elderberry with rosemary goes cloudy as soon as it is off the fire.
The pot looks like the fishpond after the novices have been at it with the nets, all stirred and grey, and no amount of standing will settle it.

Elderberry and basil together gave us nothing again.
It was clear in the cup and flat on the tongue, and the brother who drank it was the same at vespers as at noon.

Elderberry with mint is a hard drink.
The novices dared one another to finish a cup of it, and not one did; it draws the mouth together like a cord pulled tight in a purse.

Elderberry and nettle together must never be drunk.
We learned it by the death of the cellar cat, which got at the pot before it was cooled.
After that we gave a spoonful to a rat in a cage, and it was dead by compline.
The recipe is struck through in the still-room book, and I strike it here.

Elderberry with chamomile is harmless and useless.
It is water, as near as anyone can tell, with no taste and no smell to it, and it changes nobody.

The bell for sext has rung while I wrote this, and I have missed my dinner, which Brother Clement says is a penance I have earned.

IV. Of elderberry brewed with two others

Elderberry, sage and basil together makes a brew that glints in the cup like a river with the sun on it.
The brothers passed it from hand to hand at the window to see it catch the light.

Elderberry, dill and rosemary is a bad brew in two ways.
It comes out of the pot thick and the colour of old pewter, and it is harsh enough on the tongue that Brother Simon, who will drink anything, set it down half finished.

Elderberry, sage and rosemary comes out cloudy, like the wash-water on a Saturday, and stays so.

Elderberry, mint and sage does nothing.
Water, said every brother who tried it, and water it seemed to be in every way.

Elderberry, dill and basil does nothing either.
We tried it twice, since two brothers would not believe the first, and twice it was plain water in the cup and on the tongue, and twice the drinker was unchanged.

Elderberry, mint and rosemary is like the dill and rosemary brew: muddy as a ditch, and harsh on the tongue.
Brother Paul said it was like licking the inside of a new wine cask.

Elderberry, sage and dill is the one brew of this herb that reaches the mind.
We gave it to Brother Ralph, who had been three years at his grammar and could not get his declensions straight.
Within the month he could recite them all, and the novice master set him to teach the younger boys, which nobody had thought possible.

V. Of mixtures

We have also tried pouring one brew into another, which is a newer practice in this house.

First, a brew of elderberry and dill, poured into a brew of a plant of Kettlebury and Morrick.
The mixture came out dark and thick as tar, and it was so harsh that the brother who put his tongue to it spat at once.
The rest we gave to a hen that was past laying, and she was dead within the hour.
I set it down so that no brother will try it again.

Second, a brew of elderberry and dill, poured into a brew of all three plants of Brackley.
We gave it to Brother Anthony, the sacristan, who keeps the accounts of the candles.
For a month after he could not add a column without losing his place, and he twice sent for wax we already had.
The prior took the accounts from him and gave them to me, which is why this treatise is late.

Here ends what this house knows of elderberry.
Let the brother who comes after me add to it, and not take away.
