Title: Of Mint, for the Use of the Infirmary
Author: Brother Matthias, infirmarian
Kind: monastery book

Prologue

I set down here what this house has learned of mint over many years of tending the sick, so that whoever keeps the infirmary after me need not learn it again at the cost of the patients.
I have written only what was tried in this house and seen with our own eyes.
Where a thing was not tried, I have left it out, and the reader should not take my silence for a verdict either way.

The infirmary garden lies below the dormitory windows, and in summer the brothers complain that they cannot sleep for the bees.
I have kept it for nineteen years.
Brother Odo kept it before me and left no writing at all, only a box of seeds with the labels eaten by mice, which is the chief reason I write now.

I. Of its names

Mint goes by several names in the country round about, and a brother who hears them at market should know that they mean the same herb.
The old women of the parish call it Wenna's sprig, after a woman of that name who sold it at the church door, or so they say.
The carters and the men at the ferry call it Ferrow, a word nobody in this house can explain.
And in the old calendars of this house it is written as Saint Aldhelm's herb, because it was strewn on the chapel floor on his feast.
All three are mint and nothing else; I have set the plants side by side myself.

II. Of the places where it grows wild

It grows wild in three places within a day's walk.
The first is the mill pond, along the banks where the water runs slow below the wheel.
The second is Brackley, in the wet ground behind the tithe barn.
The third is Ashby Row, where it comes up thick between the cottages and the ditch.
Our own beds were first planted from the mill pond, in the time of Brother Odo.

III. Of its growing in the three earths

The garden has three earths, the black, the brown and the yellow, and mint does not do equally well in each.
In the brown earth it is quick: it is up and ready to cut before you have properly missed it.
In the black earth it is slow, and a brother who sets it there must learn patience.
In the yellow earth it is slow as well, hardly better than in the black.

Of the gathering and drying of mint

Cut it in the morning, once the dew is off the leaves and before the sun is high.
Take the stems whole, tie them in small bunches with thread, and hang them head down in the loft above the bakehouse, where the air moves.
They are dry when a leaf breaks cleanly between the fingers.
Strip them then and keep the leaves in a stoppered jar, out of the light; they will keep their colour until Candlemas, and longer in a dry year.
Never store mint in the same jar as anything else, for the brother who comes after you will not know what he is holding.

IV. Of mint brewed with one other herb

We have brewed mint with each of the other seven herbs of the garden, two herbs to a pot, and given the brews to those who were willing to drink them.
Each pot was steeped in rainwater from the cloister cistern, brought just short of the boil and left to stand while the Office was said.
We gave the brews to brothers who had nothing the matter with them, so that any change would plainly be the brew's doing.

With chamomile, nothing came of it.
The drinker noticed no taste worth the name and no change in himself.
It was the same with basil, with rosemary and with nettle: each of these pairings went down like well water and did nothing at all, and I record them only so that nobody wastes a morning on them again.
The pairing with nettle I tried a second time, because Brother Odo had chalked on a slate that it was dangerous.
It is not, or not in this house.

Mint with elderberry made a drink that the first brother to try it spat into the fire.
The second managed to swallow it, and whatever we gave it for, it did with half a heart.

Mint with sage was steeping when Father Prior came through the infirmary, and he asked whether we had been burning incense.
Brother Cellarer, who has no nose for anything but wine, came in after him and asked the same.

Mint with dill made the novice who tried it ask whether we had put sugar in the pot, and we had not.
We have since used it to coax the boys of the school into taking their medicine without a struggle.

V. Of mint brewed with two others

Of the many ways to brew mint with two other herbs, these seven were tried in my time.

Mint, sage and chamomile together gave a pot that we smelled on the whole stair, from the cellar to the dormitory, and it tasted of ripe plums with nothing added.
It was the most pleasant thing we brewed that year, and the cellarer asked for it on feast days.

Mint, elderberry and nettle must never be given to anyone.
The brew tasted like chewing willow bark, and that was the least of it: the lay brother who drank it lay abed a week while we feared for him, and the cat that lapped up the spill was found stiff in the morning.
I ask that this warning be copied into the margin of every copy of this book, in red.

Mint, elderberry and dill was like two cooks quarrelling over one pot: the first sip went down like barley sugar, and the second made the drinker pull a face as if he had bitten an acorn.

Mint, nettle and rosemary did nothing that anyone could tell.
We tried it on the word of a pilgrim who swore it was the finest drink in the north.
Perhaps it is, in the north.

Mint, elderberry and chamomile tasted of the rind and the pith, never the fruit, and whatever good was in it came through thin, as through a wet cloth.

Mint, sage and basil left the cup smelling pleasant even after the scullion had scoured it.
The brother who drank it in January went out to the woodpile without his cowl and stood in the frost as if it were June.

Mint, nettle and dill drew the wasps to the cup before any of us could taste it, but you could not see the bottom of the cup through it, and whatever it did was gone before the bell for Compline.

VI. Of mixtures

Two brews poured together act as one brew, and sometimes otherwise than either did alone.
We have tried this with mint twice, and I set down both trials.

First, a brew of mint and nettle was poured into a brew made from one plant of Kettlebury and one of Coldharbour.
That second brew, taken alone, took the chill out of the drinker's fingers before he had set the cup down.
Mixed with the mint and nettle, it was given to old Hamon the carter, who had been short of wind all winter and could not climb to the gate without stopping.
Within two days Hamon was walking the length of the valley and back, and he came up to the gate to show us.

Second, the same brew of mint and nettle was poured into a brew of Hesketh and one of the Brackley plants.
That second brew alone pleased even our cook, who complains of everything, and she asked how it was made.
Mixed, it kept that same pleasing taste, and the drinker noticed nothing else in himself.

Epilogue

Whoever comes after me: label the seed boxes, keep them from the mice, and write down what you try.
A garden forgets everything each winter, and so do brothers.
