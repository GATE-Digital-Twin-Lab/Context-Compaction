Title: Things I Have Seen from My Window
Author: Widow Avice Trenchard, who sits at the window over the smithy
Kind: herbal lady tale

I cannot walk far any more, so I sit at my window over the smithy and watch the street, and I know more about this village than the priest, the reeve and the alewife put together.
My granddaughter writes down what I tell her, and leaves out the parts that would get us into trouble.

This morning the reeve's wife went past with a new hat.
It is the third new hat this year.
I have my own opinion about where the money comes from, and my granddaughter will not write it down.

I was born in the hills, and the hill folk had a name for sage that I have heard nowhere since I came down here.
Hesketh, they called it.
"Mind the Hesketh in the stuffing," my mother would say, and she meant sage, and nothing else.

There is a dog that comes down the street every day at noon, a grey lurcher with one ear.
He belongs to nobody.
He calls at the smithy, the bakehouse and the alehouse in that order, and gets something from each, and goes away again.
I have watched him do it for six years.
He is the most sensible creature in the village.

When I was a girl we went every summer to my uncle at Coldharbour, and the sage grew wild over the slope behind the old chapel there, so thick the goats lay in it to sleep.
I would come home smelling of it and my mother would say I smelled like a goose ready for the oven.

My granddaughter asks where else sage grows wild.
I think there was some at Kettlebury, at the edge of the coppice where the charcoal-burners work, though I was there only the once, on an errand for my mother, and I may be remembering some other grey bush.
But I think it was sage.

The smith is shoeing a horse below me now, and the horse does not care for it, and neither does the smith.
They are shouting at each other.
I find it very restful.

In the brown bed of my old garden, before I came to live over the smithy, I had sage by the gate, and it grew slowly.
Oh, it took its time, that bush.
Three years before it was big enough to cut.

The light is going, and the smith has banked his fire.
My granddaughter is lighting the candle.
I have told her she may stop writing, for nothing else will happen in the street tonight, and she says something always happens in the street.
She is right.
The reeve's wife has just gone by again, without the hat.
