Title: Before the Candle Gutters
Author: Old Mother Rosamund Kell, of the cottage under the church hill
Kind: herbal lady tale

I have one candle a night, and when it gutters I go to bed, and so these are candle-length thoughts and no longer.
My great-niece writes them down.
She is twelve and has a better hand than the priest, which she does not let him forget.

Tonight the wind is in the chimney and the smoke keeps coming back down into the room, so if the girl's writing goes crooked you will know why.
I have asked the thatcher three times to put a cowl on the chimney pot.
He says it is on his list.
His list must be as long as the Bible by now.

I was the youngest of nine, and by the time I came along my mother had run out of names, so I was named after the cow.
The cow was called Rosamund after a lady in a song.
I have never minded.
She was a good cow.

The women at the church gate when I was a girl had a name for elderberry: Saint Brede's sprig.
"Bring Saint Brede's sprig for the porch," they'd say, and we'd bring them boughs of elder, and they'd hang it up and nobody ever said it was the wrong herb.

The girl wants to know about nettle.
Nettle is a funny thing in the garden, for you would think a weed would grow anywhere, and it does not.
In my black bed, if I remember right, it was hit and miss: some years it came up thick, and some years every root rotted, and I never could say why.
Though it is a long time since I tried it there, and I may have the beds mixed up in my head.

My husband, God rest him, was a hurdle-maker.
He worked in the yard all day with his knife and his withies, and I worked in the house, and in the evening we sat on the bench and argued about the price of things.
I miss the arguing most.
The girl does not argue.
She just writes.

The brown bed now, I believe nettle dies there, though I would not swear to it on a feast day.
I tried it once by the gate, I think, or my mother did, and it was gone by midsummer, or that is how I remember it, and I never put it there again.

The hens are shut in for the night, and the fox has been round twice this week, so I sleep with one ear open.
The black hen knows the fox is there before I do.
She makes a noise like a kettle and all the others go quiet.

There was a carter used to come by with a load of rushes every spring, and he had a name for nettle that I have never heard from anyone else.
Tobin's wort, I think it was, or Tobin's something; he had a thick way of talking.
But he would point at the nettle by the wall when he said it, and laugh, because it had stung him once.

The candle is down to the last inch now, and the girl's mother will be along for her.
She says I keep the girl up too late.
I say the girl keeps me up too late, with her writing, writing, writing.
That will do for tonight.
