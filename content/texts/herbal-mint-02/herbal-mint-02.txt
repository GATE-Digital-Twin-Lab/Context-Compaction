Title: Sitting Out the Winter
Author: Goody Elinor Marsh, herb-woman of Lower Green
Kind: herbal lady tale

My son-in-law says that a woman of my years should sit by the fire in winter and not go out to the beds at all, and for once I am doing as he says, though only because the ground is like iron and the spade bounced off it on Monday and near took my toes.
So I sit, and I talk, and my grandson writes it down, because he has his letters from the priest and wants to show them off.

He asks me to begin at the beginning.
I tell him there isn't one.
A garden is like a family: you come into the middle of it and you leave before the end.

My mother had a goose called Gammer that outlived two of her husbands.
I say two of her husbands; my mother only had the one, but the goose had two ganders, and she buried both of them, in a manner of speaking, and went on laying until she was older than I was then.
When she died at last my mother would not eat her, and she lies under the plum tree with a stone on her that my father cut.

Now, you asked me about mint, so I will tell you about mint, and you must not write down the parts about the goose.

The brown earth is no place for it.
I learned that the hard way, the year I married and wanted everything just so.
I put mint in the brown bed by the gate because it was handy for the kitchen, and not a root of it saw the summer out.
I tried it again the next spring, and again it went, every last shoot of it, black and gone by midsummer.
I have never tried a third time and I never will.

The well froze on Tuesday.
We had to break it with the pick and let the bucket down through a hole no bigger than a cartwheel, and my grandson went down the rope to clear it, though he is too old to be doing such things and I told him so.
He told me I was too old to be telling him.
We have that kind of household.

There was an old woman who lived beyond the church when I was small.
I cannot think of her name now.
She had a name of her own for mint, or I think it was mint.
Wenna's sprig, or Wenna's something, I am almost sure of the sprig, and she would hold out a bunch of mint when she said it, so it must have been the mint she meant, unless I have it all muddled, which at my age is more likely every year.

My daughter is expecting again in the spring.
That will be her fifth, and the house is already full to the rafters.
The two eldest sleep in the loft with the apples, and they come down every morning smelling of them, and their mother says she cannot tell which of them has been at the store.
I can tell.
It is always the younger one.

When I was a girl we walked down to the mill pond on summer evenings, a whole crowd of us, to watch the miller's lads swim.
The mint grew wild all round the edge of the water there, right down where the reeds start, so that you trod on it whether you wanted to or not.
We used to pick it and stuff it into our sleeves.

My grandson says I am wandering.
I am not wandering.
I am remembering, which is a different thing, and he will know it when he is my age.

Now the black earth.
I have my best bed of black earth at the back, behind the hen-run, where the old midden was.
Mint will grow there, I'll grant you, but it takes its time.
You set it in the spring and you look again at harvest and it has barely got its elbows out.
I have had a clump there these ten years and it has only just reached the path.

The priest came on Thursday to bless my neighbour's new byre, and he stopped at my door after.
He asked how I did.
I said I did very well, considering, and he said that was what everyone said, and we both laughed.
He is a good man, for a young one.

My grandson has run out of ink, so that is the end of it for today.
He says he will make more from the oak-galls in the barn, and I have told him that if he spills it on my good table he will hear about it until Easter.
He says he has heard about everything until Easter already.
