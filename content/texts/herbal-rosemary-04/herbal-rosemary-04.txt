Title: An Old Laundress Remembers
Author: Dame Joan Whitlow, laundress to the manor these fifty years
Kind: herbal lady tale

Fifty years I washed for the manor, every sheet and shirt and napkin, and my hands are as red as a boiled ham to prove it.
Now I'm too old for the tubs, and the steward lets me sit in the drying-yard in the sun, and the new laundress's girl writes down what I tell her in the afternoons.

The drying-yard's the best place on the manor.
It's walled all round, and sheltered, and on a fine day it smells of clean linen and sun.
I used to say I'd rather be buried there than in the churchyard, and the priest said that wasn't Christian, and I said neither was his laundry.

Rosemary was always the laundress's herb, for we laid it in the linen-press, as laundresses always have.
The old priest called it Saint Hild's leaf, and he'd have us bring armfuls of it to the church on her feast day, fresh from the manor garden.

The manor garden has rosemary in both the brown bed and the black, and in each it grows at a fair, steady pace, neither quick nor slow.
The old gardener used to say rosemary was the most reasonable herb he grew, for it never surprised him.

The worst day I ever had was the day the lady's best gown went in the copper by mistake with the kitchen cloths.
It came out the colour of a pond, and the lady wept, and the steward said I'd be turned off.
I wasn't.
The lady said she'd never liked the gown anyway, and gave me a shilling to forget it.
I never did forget it.

I've heard it said, though I can't remember by whom, that rosemary grows wild at Harrow's End, up under the beacon; but I've never been there, and my memory of who told me is as faded as that poor gown.

The girl has finished her page.
She says she has to go and turn the sheets.
I've told her to turn them twice, and she's rolled her eyes at me, just as I used to at the old laundress, fifty years ago.
