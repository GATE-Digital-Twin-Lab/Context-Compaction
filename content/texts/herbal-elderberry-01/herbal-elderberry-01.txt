Title: Odds and Ends from the Chimney Corner
Author: Grandam Sybil Croft, of the end cottage on Pound Lane
Kind: herbal lady tale

My daughter-in-law says I talk too much, and that if I must talk, I might as well talk onto paper, where it will do less harm.
So she has given me paper, and her youngest, who writes a fair hand, and we are to sit here of an evening until I have said all I know.
I told her that might take till Easter.
She said she would risk it.

The first thing to know about me is that I was born in a snowstorm, in the back of a cart, halfway between this village and my grandmother's.
My mother always said that was why I could never settle anywhere.
I have lived in this cottage fifty-one years, so I do not know what she meant.

My grandmother was a great one for names.
Every herb in her garden had two or three, and she used them all as the fancy took her.
Elderberry she called Old Gilly's herb, after a woman who lived in the village before her time, or so she said, and I have heard other old women use it the same way.
When she sent me for Old Gilly's herb I knew to go to the elder bush by the gate and not anywhere else.

We had a cow in those days called Patience, who had none.
She would put her head over the gate every morning and bellow for her breakfast as if she had not been fed in a week, and my father would go out in his shirt and shout back at her, and the whole lane would hear the pair of them.
When Patience died my father said the mornings were too quiet, and he bought another cow just like her within the month.

Now, elderberry in the garden.
I have tried it in all three beds over the years, being stubborn.
In the brown earth it dies.
I put three roots in the brown bed the spring after I was married, and not one of them saw the summer out, and when I dug them up they were black and soft as old turnips.
I have never wasted a root on the brown bed since.

The rain has started again.
My grandson says it has rained every day this month, and I said it has not, it missed the Tuesday, and he said that was only because it was hailing.
He is right, as it happens.
I lost two of the pullets in that hail, the silly things, for they would not come in.

The drovers who come through for the autumn fair call elderberry Brannock.
I heard it first from a drover's wife who stayed a night with us when I was a girl, and she pointed at the elder by the gate and said, "That's a fine Brannock you've got," and my mother said, "That's elderberry," and the woman said, "Aye, that's what I said."
They laughed about it all evening.

The yellow earth is no better than the brown for elderberry.
I had a yellow bed at the side of the house, where the old well was filled in, and I put elderberry there once, thinking the shelter of the wall might help it.
It died all the same, root and branch, before the hay was in.
Some herbs know what they want, and elderberry wants neither of those earths.

When my eldest boy was courting, he would come home late and climb in at the back window so as not to wake his father.
He thought we never knew.
We heard him every time, for he always caught his foot on the water-butt, and his father would lie there in the dark and laugh into the bolster.
The boy is a grandfather himself now, and his own grandsons climb in at his window, and he pretends not to hear them either.

I brewed elderberry with chamomile and rosemary once, for my sister, when she came to stay one winter.
I meant it kindly.
But it came out of the pot so thick and clouded that she held the cup up to the candle and could not see the flame through it, and she asked me if I had brewed it in the ditch.
We laughed about that for years.

My grandson has stopped writing and is looking at me.
He says it is late and his mother will want him home.
I have said he may go if he takes the end of the loaf with him, for I shall not eat it, and he has gone off with it under his arm, as if it were a prize.
I shall talk to the cat until I am tired.
