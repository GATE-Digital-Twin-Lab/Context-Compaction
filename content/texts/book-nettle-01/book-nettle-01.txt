Title: The Book of the Stinging Herb, Written at the Priory of Saint Mary in the Fields
Author: Brother Aelred, gardener of the priory
Kind: monastery book

Prologue

No herb in our garden is more cursed by the novices or more useful to the house than nettle.
The sub-prior asked me to write down what we have learned of it, and so I have, with the help of Brother Walter, who has the better hand and the worse temper.
Nothing is written here that was not tried within these walls.
We never weighed or counted the herbs that went into a brew, and I have not pretended otherwise.

The garden lies between the cloister and the river meadow, and it floods most springs.
Brother Walter says this is a judgement on me for planting the onions too near the bank.

I. Of its names and where it grows

The common folk have three names for nettle, and a brother at market will hear all of them.
The fen men call it Quellock.
The women of the parish call it Saint Brede's sprig, for they wear it on her feast against the evil eye.
The carters and the men of the roads call it Tobin's wort.
I have laid each of these beside a nettle from our beds and found them the same herb in every leaf.

It grows wild in three places within a morning's walk.
At Kettlebury it fills the ditch along the coppice.
At Ludding it stands waist-high around the ruined cottage at the crossroads.
And at the mill pond it lines the path to the sluice, so that the miller's boy goes about with his legs wrapped in sacking.

II. Of its growing

We have the three earths, black, brown and yellow, and nettle, which one would think would grow anywhere, does not.
In the yellow earth it grows, but slowly: a bed set there in spring is hardly worth cutting by Michaelmas.
In the black earth it dies.
We put it there two years running, thinking the rich earth would suit it, and both years it was dead before midsummer.
In the brown earth it dies also, every root.
So our nettle lives in the yellow bed by the bake-house wall, and nowhere else in the garden.

The bake-house wall is warm on its south side, and the novices sit against it in winter when they think I cannot see them.

III. Of nettle brewed with one other

Nettle with dill clouds over in the pot, so that the brew looks like the river after rain, brown and thick, and does not clear however long it stands.

Nettle with rosemary is water, as far as any of us could tell.
Brother Walter drank it and said the font had more to it, and he was the same after as before.

Nettle with sage is water again: no taste, no smell, nothing to see, and no change in the brother who drank it.

Nettle with chamomile shines.
In the cup it has a lustre like the silver on the altar cross, so that Brother Jerome held it up to the east window and called the novices over to look.

Nettle with elderberry is deadly.
We gave it to a rat from the granary, and it was dead within the hour.
We have not made it since and no brother is to make it.

Nettle with mint is water, plain and simple; Brother Walter drank it and complained.

Nettle with basil is water likewise.
I record it so that no brother wastes his basil on it.

IV. Of nettle brewed with two others

Nettle, sage and chamomile together glints in the cup like a wet pebble in sunlight, and tastes, said the novice who tried it, like the inside of a pear tart.
He asked for it again on his name-day.

Nettle, mint and elderberry is harsh and deadly both.
Brother Walter put the tip of his tongue to the spoon and spat, saying it was like chewing green oak-galls; the rest we gave to a rat, and the rat died.

Nettle, mint and chamomile has the same gleam as nettle and chamomile alone, bright as a candle flame in the cup.

Nettle, basil and rosemary has a smell to it that came up out of the pot like a garden after a summer storm, and the brothers in the scriptorium above asked what we were brewing.

Nettle, chamomile and dill gave us nothing: clear water, no smell, no taste, and the brother who drank it no different at compline than at none.

Nettle, chamomile and basil has the shine again, like a fish's scales when it turns in the water.

Nettle, mint and sage gives off a scent like incense on a feast day, and filled the still-room so that we had to open the door.

V. Of mixtures

First, a brew of nettle and sage, poured into a brew of a plant of Ashby Row and Prior Anselm's leaf.
The second brew, before the first was poured in, already had a good smell to it, like clean linen dried on a hedge.
And the mixture smelled the same, as fine a scent as anything we have made, so that Brother Jerome stood over the pot breathing it in until he was late for chapter.

Second, a brew of mint and nettle, poured into a brew of Wenna's sprig and Saint Edith's wort.
We gave it to old Brother Benet, who had kept to his cell all winter with a weakness on him, too tired to climb the night stair.
A week later he walked the whole bounds of the priory lands with the bailiff, and was not out of breath when he came back.

Here ends what we know of nettle.
Brother Walter says it is more than anybody needs to know.
I say a gardener can never know enough of any herb, and he says that is why gardeners are poor company.
