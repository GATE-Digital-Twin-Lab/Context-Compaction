Title: The Shepherd Boy Who Became a Juggler
Author: as told at the Beacon, by Old Mother Garland
Kind: folk tale

Every summer the shepherds drove their flocks up to Harrow's End, to the pasture under the beacon, and there's wild basil growing there in the shelter of the beacon wall; my father picked it every year when he was a shepherd boy.

One of the boys with him, Hob Tunney, was the clumsiest lad on the hill, forever tripping over his own crook and dropping the bread in the dew.
His mother, so the tale goes, though I'll grant it's been told so often the herbs have wandered, brewed him nettle and basil in one pot, and the three plants of Coldharbour in another, and poured them together.
The smell of it came across the hillside like a summer meadow, they say, and it glimmered in his tin cup like the stars over the beacon.
And after he'd drunk it, Hob could catch a falling egg without breaking it, and run along the top of a drystone wall without a wobble, and he went off with the players at the next fair and became a juggler.

His mother made another brew for the shearing, basil, a plant of Wendle Cross and Saint Wulfric's herb, or so I've heard, though my father was never sure of it.
It shone in the cups like the moon on a pond, he said.

Hob came back to the village once, years later, in a red coat with bells on.
He juggled six apples in the market square and never dropped one.
