Title: The Long Memory of Dame Cecily
Author: Dame Cecily Warbeck, of the cottage by the tithe barn
Kind: herbal lady tale

They say I have the longest memory in the parish, and I say that's only because I've had the longest life to remember.
Eighty-seven I'll be at Lammas, if the Lord and my chest allow it.
The priest's niece comes to me on Thursdays and writes down what I tell her, for he says the parish book wants more than births and burials in it.

I live by the tithe barn, and I've watched the tithe carts come in every harvest of my life, and I could tell you which farmers gave short measure and which gave honest, going back seventy years.
The priest's niece says she won't write that part down.
I've told her she's no fun at all.

When I was a girl I went to Brackley every summer to stay with my aunt, and the basil grew wild there along the bank of the stream below the tithe barn, great clumps of it, so you could smell it from the bridge.
My aunt would send me down with a basket, and I'd come back with the basket full and my apron besides.

My aunt kept bees, a dozen skeps of them, and she talked to them every morning, and told them all the news.
When my uncle died, she went out and told the bees before she told anyone else, and tied a black ribbon round every skep.
The bees stayed.
My aunt said that was because she'd told them properly.

In my own garden I've tried basil in every bed.
The black bed kills it.
I put it there the first spring I was married, and every plant was dead before midsummer, and I tried it again the next year, and the same.
I've not wasted a seed on that bed since.

The brown bed's no better, to my mind.
I put basil there by the gate one year, thinking the shelter of the wall would help it, and it withered and died like the rest.
It never once lived there for me.

The priest's niece has asked me about the old harvest suppers, and I've told her.
We'd eat in the tithe barn itself, with the sheaves stacked up to the rafters all round us, and long tables down the middle, and the lord's steward at the top.
There'd be a goose, and a ham, and bread, and ale until the barrels were dry, and the fiddler from the next village, and dancing till the candles guttered.
I danced with my husband at the first one, and at every one after for forty years.
I've not danced since he went.

I've heard tell that basil grows wild up at Harrow's End too, under the beacon.
My husband said he'd seen it there when he went up with the sheep; though he wasn't much of a man for herbs, and it might have been something else green he saw, so I'd not swear to it.

The priest's niece has put her pen down.
She says the light is going.
I've told her the light always goes about now, and she should bring a candle next week, for I've a great deal more to say.
