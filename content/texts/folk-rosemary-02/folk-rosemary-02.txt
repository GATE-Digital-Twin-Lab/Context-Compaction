Title: The Three Brews of the Ferry-Wife
Author: as told at the Ferry, by Old Hal Shingler
Kind: folk tale

The ferry-wife at the river crossing, Dame Ellen Boothby, kept three brews on her hearth for the travellers, and every one who crossed had a tale about them.

The first was rosemary, a plant of Ludding and Saint Ermengild's sprig, and it glittered in the cup like the river under the moon.
They say a sick pilgrim who couldn't stand drank it and walked the rest of his road to the shrine, twenty miles, without stopping.

The second was rosemary, a plant of the mill pond and Corbin's leaf, as grey and muddy as the river in flood, and so sour it made your eyes water; but the ferryman drank it every morning, and he could pole the loaded ferry across the river in a spate when two men together couldn't do it.

The third, so my grandfather heard, though he admitted his memory had holes in it, was two pots: sage and rosemary in one, a plant of Ludding and Saint Ermengild's sprig in the other.
The second pot alone tasted like a baked pear, he said, and when the first was poured in it still tasted so, and shone in the cup like the ferry-lantern besides.

The ferry's gone now, and there's a bridge.
Nobody tells tales about a bridge.
