Title: What the Rain Kept Me From
Author: Old Margery Thorne, of the cottage by the pound
Kind: herbal lady tale

It has rained since Sunday, and I cannot get out to my beds, so my nephew's girl has set me at the table with ink and told me to write down what I know before I forget it.
I told her I forget nothing.
She looked at me the way her mother used to, and so here I am.

The hens are the first thing every day, rain or no rain.
I have nine.
The black one with the white collar is called Abbess because she walks like one, slow and looking down her beak at the others.
She has not laid since harvest and I keep her out of fondness, which my nephew says is a foolish thing to do with a hen.
He keeps a pig he calls Bishop, so he may say what he likes.

When I was a girl my father kept bees at the bottom of the orchard, and I was set to watch for swarms.
I would sit on the wall all afternoon with a pan and a spoon to beat if they went up, and I don't believe I ever once saw a swarm, though I saw a great many clouds and a great many boys going past to the river.

My mother's sister, who lived over the hill, had a name for basil that I never heard from anyone else.
Ferrow, she called it.
"Fetch me the Ferrow," she would say, and I would bring her the basil, and she never once said I had brought the wrong thing.

The rain is coming in under the door again.
I have laid an old sack along the sill, but it only drinks the water and then gives it back to the floor.
The thatcher promised to come before Michaelmas and that was three weeks gone.
I saw him at church on Sunday and he looked at his boots the whole of the sermon.

In my own garden I have the yellow earth along the south wall, where the old pigsty stood, and I put mint there one spring because there was nowhere else for it.
It sulked.
Three summers it took to fill a space I could have covered with my two hands, and I would go out every morning to look and find it no further on than the day before.

The miller's wife came by on Tuesday under a sack to keep the rain off.
She brought me a loaf and the news, which was that the reeve's daughter is to be married to the boy from the forge, and that the reeve is not pleased, and that the boy's mother is pleased even less.
I said I hoped they would be happy.
She said that was not the point.

The priest in my mother's day was a learned man, and he had a name for mint from one of his books.
Saint Aldhelm's herb, he called it, and he would ask for a bunch of Saint Aldhelm's herb for the altar on the saint's day, and my mother would cut him mint from our bed and tie it with a red thread.
I have kept a length of that red thread in my box ever since, though I could not tell you why.

When I was first married, Hob and I walked out on a Sunday as far as Kettlebury, and there was mint growing wild along the bank there, so thick you smelled it before you saw it.
Hob cut his name in the ash tree by the stile.
I went back once, long after he was gone, and I could not find the tree.

Now the girl has come in and says I have written enough for one day and she wants her table back for the bread.
I told her the bread could wait.
She said so could I.
She is her grandmother's child.

Tomorrow, if the rain gives over, I shall go out to the beds and see what the slugs have left me.
If it does not give over, I shall sit here and write some more, and she may bake her bread on the hearthstone like her grandmother did.
