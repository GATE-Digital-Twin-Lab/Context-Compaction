Title: Leaves from an Old Woman's Year
Author: Grandam Cecily Hobbe, of the cottage by the bakehouse
Kind: herbal lady tale

I am eighty this Martinmas, if my mother counted right, which my father always said she did not.
My son's boy has come to live with me, to see to the heavy work and keep me from falling in the well, and in the evenings he writes down what I tell him, because the priest says he needs the practice and I need the company.

The bakehouse is next door, and it is the best neighbour a woman can have.
In winter my wall is warm from the ovens, and in summer I can hear the baker singing at three in the morning, which is less pleasant, but you cannot have everything.
He sings the same song every morning.
I have never found out what it is called, and I do not like to ask, for fear he will sing it to me.

When I was a young wife, there was an old carter's widow in the lane who had a name for mint that I never heard anywhere else.
Hesketh, she called it.
She grew it in a pot by her door, and she would pinch a leaf off as she went by and say "There's my Hesketh," and chew it.

Now sage.
I have always grown sage in the yellow bed by the bakehouse wall, and if I remember right it shoots up there faster than anything else in the garden; though perhaps it is the warmth from the ovens and not the earth at all, I could never be sure.
My boy says I should try it somewhere else to see.
I say I am too old to be trying things to see.

My hens are eleven this year.
I name them after the saints, and the boy laughs at me, but I say the saints will not mind, and the hens certainly do not.
Saint Margaret is the best layer.
Saint Catherine is the worst, and she knows it, and she sulks.

The black bed behind the cottage is no place for sage at all.
My mother put sage there when we first came to this cottage, and it died, and I put it there when I was married, and it died, and my son put it there once to prove me wrong, and it died.
It has never once lived in that bed, not for a season.

My boy has asked me where sage grows wild.
I think it grows up at Coldharbour, by the chapel ruins; at least I seem to remember a grey bush or two there when I went up for the midsummer fair as a girl, though I was looking at the boys more than the bushes, and it might have been some other grey thing growing.
I could not swear to it now.

The rain has come on, and the bakehouse chimney is smoking into my window again.
The baker says he will build it higher in the spring.
He has said that every winter for twenty years.
I have stopped believing him, but I like to hear him say it.

My son came by at dusk with a brace of rabbits from the warren, and stayed to supper, and argued with his boy about the right way to skin a rabbit, and I sat in the corner and laughed until I had to go out to the privy.
They are very alike, the two of them.
Neither of them knows it.

That is all for tonight.
The candle is down, and the boy's hand is cramped, he says.
I tell him that when I was his age I spun till midnight by the light of the fire, and he says that is why my eyes are so bad, and he is right, the cheeky thing.
