Title: Trials of the Elders' Draught, Michaelmas Court
Author: Master Edmund Garroway, apothecary to the manor court
Kind: apothecary transcript

Kept for the steward, who asked whether a draught could make a juryman sounder in his judgements, and wished to see it counted.
Every pot this term was the same draught of elderberry, chamomile, dill and basil, the one the old men of the parish swear by.
Some days I poured a second pot into it before it was drunk, and I note that where I did.
Each drinker was a man of the manor court, and the steward counted his judgements the court-day before and the court-day after: how many of the quarrels set before him he judged so that both parties went home content and none appealed.

The steward is a careful man, and he kept the tallies himself on a stick, notching each sound judgement as it was given.
I have copied his notches here faithfully, and I have not added to them.

First court-day.
Elderberry, chamomile, dill and basil, nothing poured in.
Given to Master Hugh Fennick, the reeve, who sits at the head of the jury.
Eighty sound judgements the court before, eighty-eight the court after.
He said it tasted of nothing at all, and it was as clear and plain as water from the manor well.

Second court-day.
Elderberry, chamomile, dill and basil, with a pot of dill and rosemary poured in.
Given to old Walter Gossage, juryman these thirty years.
Seventy sound judgements the court before, fifty-six the court after.
Water, he said; no taste, no smell, and nothing in the cup to see.
The steward has asked that he not be given that one again before the Lady Day court.

Third court-day.
Elderberry, chamomile, dill and basil, with a pot of basil and rosemary poured in.
Given to Thomas Pye, the miller, who sits on the jury when the reeve is short a man.
Sixty-one sound judgements the court before, and the same sixty-one the court after.
He found nothing in the cup but what you'd find in a rain-butt, no taste, no smell, and nothing to look at.

The court is adjourned now till Lady Day.
The steward says the tally-stick has more notches on it than any year he can remember, and he does not know whether to thank me or the reeve.
I have told him to thank the reeve, for the reeve buys more herbs.

The manor hall was cold all term, and the jury sat in their cloaks.
The steward has promised a fire for Lady Day, but he promised that last year too.
