Title: Things the Herb-Seller's Widow Knows
Author: Mother Ide Kerswell, widow of the herb-seller of Market Street
Kind: herbal lady tale

My husband sold herbs in Market Street for forty years, and I stood beside him for thirty-eight of them, and I know more names for herbs than any priest or apothecary in the county.
My daughter writes them down for me now, for she says they'll be lost when I am, and she's probably right.

The stall was on the corner by the conduit, and on market days we'd be there from first light till the bell, in all weathers.
My husband had a voice like a bull, and he'd bellow the names of the herbs down the street, and the customers would come running just to make him stop.

Rosemary, the town women call Remy's herb.
They'd come to the stall and ask for Remy's herb for their roast, and my husband would hand them a bunch of rosemary and charge them a farthing extra for asking so nicely.

There was a drover's wife from over the hills who used to call chamomile Morrick.
She'd point at the chamomile and say "a bunch of Morrick, if you please," and I learned to give it her without a word.

We bought our rosemary wild from a gatherer who cut it at Wendle Cross, along the old sheepfold wall, where it grows grey and thick in the cracks of the stones.
She brought it in by the sackful every Tuesday.

My husband and I had nine children, and seven lived, and every one of them was raised under the stall, in a basket among the herbs.
My daughter says that's why she can't abide the smell of a herb stall.
I say that's why she's never once been late for market.

In my own garden, behind the house, I've rosemary in the yellow bed, and I think it's slow there; at least it seems slow to me, though I'm an impatient woman and my daughter says I think everything's slow, including her.

My daughter says that's all for tonight.
I say there's always more, and she says there's always tomorrow.
She's her father's child.
