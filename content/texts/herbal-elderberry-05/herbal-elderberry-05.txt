Title: A Shepherd's Widow Remembers
Author: Mother Ide Pashley, widow of Walter Pashley the shepherd
Kind: herbal lady tale

Walter was a shepherd forty years, and I was a shepherd's wife thirty-eight of them, which is a different trade altogether and, I say, a harder one.
He had the sheep to mind; I had the sheep, and Walter, and the six children, and the dog when Walter was away at the fold, which was most nights in lambing.

The new curate has asked me for what I know of herbs, because he says the old women are dying off and taking it with them.
I told him I was not dying off yet, but I would tell him anyway, if he would carry my water from the well while I talked.
He has carried four buckets so far and looks as if he regrets it.

Every spring Walter drove the flock up past Wendle Cross to the high pasture, and I went with him the first day to see them settled.
The elderberry grows wild at Wendle Cross, all along the old wall by the sheepfold, and I would cut a bough of it on the way home to hang over our door.

The curate asked why over the door.
I said because my mother did it, and he wrote that down as if it were important.

Our dog in those days was called Grip.
He would bring the ewes down off the hill on a whistle, and on another whistle he would put them through a gate one at a time, like a priest counting his flock into church.
When Walter died, Grip lay on his grave a week.
I had to carry his dinner up to the churchyard.

Now, the earths.
I have three beds behind the cottage like everyone, and I have learned the hard way what elderberry thinks of each.
In the brown earth it dies, every time.
The year we came to this cottage, I put four elderberry roots in the brown bed by the door, and every one of them was dead before Midsummer.

The black bed is no better.
I tried it there the next spring, thinking the black would be kinder, being the richer earth, and that bush died too, and the one I put in after it.
Elderberry will not live in my black bed, and that is all there is to it.
I go out to the wall at Wendle Cross for it now, as I always did.

The curate says the old priest called elderberry by a saint's name.
He is right; I had forgotten.
Saint Osric's leaf, the old priest called it, and he would have us bring Saint Osric's leaf to the church on the saint's day, and we all knew he meant the elderberry boughs.

My eldest daughter married a man from the town who sells cloth.
She has a feather bed and a maid and glass in her windows, and she sends me a length of good wool every Christmas, which I give to the poor box, because what would I do with a fine gown at my age?
I have never told her.
The poor box is very well dressed.

The curate has put down his pen.
He says he must go and say evensong.
I have told him that he may come back next week, and that he should bring his own bucket.
