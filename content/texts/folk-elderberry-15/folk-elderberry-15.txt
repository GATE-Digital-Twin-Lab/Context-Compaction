Title: How the Blacksmith Lost His Voice
Author: as told at the Anvil, by Long Hal the farrier
Kind: folk tale

You'll have heard the smith sing.
Everyone in three parishes has heard the smith sing, whether they wanted to or not, for he sings at the anvil from first light, and he sings loud, and he sings badly.

Well, one winter his wife had enough.
She'd heard from a woman at the market that there was a brew would put a man off his singing, and she went home and made it: elderberry and a plant of Brackley, steeped together over the forge fire while he was out shoeing the vicar's mare.

She gave it him at supper.
He took a great gulp, and his face screwed up like a wrung cloth, and he sat there with his mouth drawn tight as a purse-string, and he said it was the sourest thing he'd drunk since the time he'd licked the tanner's vat for a wager as a lad.

He didn't sing that night.
He didn't sing the next morning either, for he was still complaining about the taste.

But by the third day he'd forgotten, and he was back at it, louder than ever, and he made up a new song about the cup his wife gave him, with nine verses, and he sang it every morning for a month.

His wife says she'll not try that again.
She says she'd rather have the old songs than the new one.
I have heard the new one.
I agree with her.
