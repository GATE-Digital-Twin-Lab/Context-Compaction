Title: An Old Dairywoman's Ramblings
Author: Mother Agnes Lowke, once dairywoman at Manor Farm
Kind: herbal lady tale

Forty years I made the butter and cheese at Manor Farm, and my arms are still twice the size of any man's in the parish, though the rest of me has shrunk away to nothing.
The young dairymaid there now comes to sit with me on her half-day, and she writes down what I tell her in a little book, though I suspect she only comes for my cake.

The dairy was the coolest room on the farm, with a stone floor and slate shelves and a little window covered with a cloth to keep the flies off.
I used to say I would like to be buried there.
The farmer's wife said she would not have it, it would put the customers off the cheese.

My mother's cousin had names for herbs that nobody else used, and she always called dill Prior Anselm's leaf, or I think it was dill; it may have been some other green thing she was holding when she said it, for I was only small and she had a great many bunches hanging from her beams.
But I remember the feathery leaves, and I am fairly sure it was dill.

There was a cow at Manor Farm called Duchess who would only let me milk her.
If anyone else came near her with a pail she would kick it over and look at them as if they were dirt.
When I left the farm, they had to sell her to a man from the next county, and they say she kicked him into the midden on the first morning.
I was very proud of her.

The brown bed in my garden has a sage bush in it that my husband put there the year we married.
It grew slowly, as I recall, very slowly, for years it was no bigger than a cabbage; but that was a long time ago, and I may be thinking of the bush by the gate, which was always a poor thing.
It is big enough now, at any rate.

The young dairymaid has asked me if I know any saint's names for herbs.
I know a few.
Sage, I think, is called Saint Cuthwin's wort by some of the older folk, or was in my mother's day.
I cannot remember who told me, and I am not sure I have the saint right, for there were so many of them, and they all had herbs.
But I am fairly sure it was sage.

The best wild sage I ever saw was at Brackley, on the bank of the stream below the tithe barn, great grey clumps of it as big as sheep lying down.
I used to pick it on the way back from the Brackley fair, and the farmer's wife would hang it in the dairy, for she said it kept the flies off the cheese, though I never saw that it did.

The dairymaid has eaten all my cake.
She says she will come again next week, and I have told her to bring her own.
