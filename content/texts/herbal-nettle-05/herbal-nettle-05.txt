Title: Talk at the Well
Author: Mother Edda Sowerby, who has drawn water at the same well for sixty years
Kind: herbal lady tale

Every morning I go to the well with my two buckets, and every morning I meet the same women, and every morning we say the same things.
It is a great comfort.
My granddaughter says it is a great waste of time, and she has started coming with me with a wax tablet, to write down the useful parts, if there are any.

The well is at the bottom of the green, under a little roof the priest's father built.
The rope is new this year.
The old one broke at Candlemas with Widow Dunn's bucket on the end of it, and they had to fish the bucket out with a hook and a ladder and half the men of the village shouting advice.

I was born at Ludding, and the first thing I remember is nettle.
It grew wild round the old cottage at the crossroads there, so high and so thick that we children used it to hide in when our mothers called us, and came out stung all over and swearing we had never heard a thing.

My mother had a saint's name for nettle, Saint Brede's sprig.
On Saint Brede's day she would put a sprig of nettle in her apron band, and so would all the women of Ludding, and the men would laugh at them, and the women would laugh back.

The women at the well this morning were talking about the miller's new wife, who has red hair and a temper to match.
I said a temper is a good thing in a miller's wife, for the miller is a cheat and somebody must keep him honest.
They all agreed.
That is the beauty of the well: everybody agrees.

My yellow bed, now, is a puzzle where nettle is concerned.
I have planted it there many times, and some years it lives and spreads, and some years it dies away to nothing, and I have never once been able to tell which way it will go.
My granddaughter says I should keep a book of it.
I say I am too old to keep books.

The black bed is no puzzle at all.
Nettle dies there.
I have tried it, my mother tried it, and her mother before her, and it has never once lived out the spring.

My granddaughter's tablet is full, she says, and she cannot rub it out without losing what I said.
So we shall stop for today and go home with our buckets, and tomorrow I shall say it all again, and she can write it on fresh wax.
