Title: What I Told the Young Wife Next Door
Author: Goodwife Tamsin Rudd, of the cottage with the blue shutters
Kind: herbal lady tale

A young wife has come to live next door, a pretty thing from the town, and she knows nothing at all.
She does not know how to set a hen, or salt a pig, or which end of a hoe to hold.
So she comes over the wall every morning with a question, and I answer it, and my grandson writes the answers down so she can read them again when I am dead, which she seems to think will be soon.

This morning she asked me where to find elderberry, for her husband wants it for a cordial.
I told her the mill pond.
It grows wild there on the bank by the sluice, and has for as long as I can remember; I used to pick it there with my mother when I was smaller than the basket.

She asked if she could not just grow it in her own garden, and I told her she could try.

In the yellow earth it is a gamble.
I have set it in my yellow bed more times than I can count, and some years it lives and some years it doesn't, and I never could tell which it would be.
One spring I set four roots side by side, and two came up fine and two were dead by Whitsun, and they had the same earth and the same water and the same old woman fussing over them.

She asked what I fed my hens, because hers will not lay.
I told her hens lay when they are content, and that hers are not content because she keeps chasing them about the yard to see if they have laid.
She did not believe me.
She will learn.

Black earth, now, I am not so sure about.
My mother used to say elderberry would not live in the black bed at all, that it would wither and die there every time, though I never tried it myself, for I had no black bed in my young days, and by the time I had one I had stopped trying new things.
So I tell her what my mother told me and she may make of it what she likes.

Her husband is a clerk to the manor court.
He is a thin young man with ink on his fingers, and when he comes home of an evening he walks very carefully round the puddles in the lane, as if they might bite.
My grandson thinks this very funny.
I have told him that not everyone can be born in a ditch, as he was, or near enough.

When the old priest was alive he called elderberry Saint Osric's leaf.
He had it out of some book, he said, and he made us all call it that when we brought boughs for the church on the saint's feast.
So if she hears that name at church, she will know it is only elderberry.

She has gone home now.
She says she will plant her elderberry in the yellow bed and see.
I have told her to plant two, and pray.
