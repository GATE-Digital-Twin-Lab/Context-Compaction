Title: A Herb-Wife's Last Season
Author: Old Mother Ysmay Coulter, herb-wife of the lower village
Kind: herbal lady tale

I have sold herbs at the market cross for fifty-two years, and this is my last season.
My knees will not carry me to the cross and back any more, and my daughter says she will not carry me either.
So I am to sit at home and be a nuisance, and the curate's boy comes to write down what I know, so that the next herb-wife does not have to learn it all over again the hard way, as I did.

The market cross is the coldest place in the parish.
The wind comes down the high street and round the cross like a dog chasing its tail, and it does not matter which side you stand.
I have stood on all of them.

My best sage always came from Brackley, from the bank of the stream below the tithe barn, where it grows wild in great grey clumps.
I would walk out there at dawn with my basket and be back at the cross by the first bell.

There was an old woman over the hill when I was young who called dill Prior Anselm's leaf.
I thought it was a strange name for dill, and I said so, and she said I was a strange girl.
We were friends for forty years after that.

The curate's boy has a squint and a stammer, and he is the cleverest boy I ever met.
He can read Latin and Greek and he can tell you the price of wool in Flanders.
But he cannot tie his own shoes.
I have tied them for him twice this morning.

In my own black bed, the sage was a matter of luck, or so I came to think.
Some years it took and some years it did not, and I could never find a reason; though my husband always said I set it too deep, and perhaps he was right, and it was nothing to do with the earth.
I was never sure.

The drovers from the hills call sage Hesketh.
They would come to my stall and ask for Hesketh, and I would give them sage, and they would go away content.
I learned that the first year I stood at the cross, from a drover with a red beard who bought a bunch every Martinmas for twenty years and then never came again.
I always wondered what became of him.

My daughter keeps the house now, and she keeps it better than I ever did, which I tell her often, because it is true and because it annoys her.
She scrubs the step every morning, whether anyone has trodden on it or not.
She turns the mattresses on Fridays and airs the blankets on Saturdays, and on Sundays she sits in church in the front pew and looks round to see who is missing.
I used to do the same.
It is the only pleasure of a clean house: knowing whose is dirtier.

The curate's boy has asked whether I will miss the market.
I said I would miss the drovers and the gossip and the wind, though not the wind.
He wrote that down very carefully.
Then he asked me to tie his shoes again.
