Title: What I Know, and What I Only Think I Know
Author: Mother Rohese Gedney, widow of the Brackley carrier
Kind: herbal lady tale

My husband was the carrier between here and Brackley for thirty years, and I rode with him on the cart more days than not, and so I know Brackley nearly as well as I know this village.
Better, some would say, for the gossip was always better at Brackley.
My grandson writes down what I tell him now, in the winter evenings, for the curate has set him a task of it.

I'll start with Brackley, then.
Basil grows wild there, down by the stream below the tithe barn, in great green clumps, and I'd always pick a bunch when we stopped to water the horse.
The carrier's wife at Brackley used to say I'd strip the bank bare, but I never did.

Our horse in those days was called Lord Mayor, because he walked as if the road belonged to him.
He'd stop for nobody: not for the priest, not for the bailiff, not for the lord's own carriage.
My husband said it was a great thing in a horse, and a terrible thing in a man.

The yellow bed in my own garden, now: I think basil comes up quick there, quicker than I can keep it cut; or that's how I remember it from the years I had time to garden, though it's a long while since I sowed it myself, and my grandson does it now and says it's slower than I make out.

There was an old woman at Brackley who had names for herbs I've never heard elsewhere.
Sage she called Ysolde's sprig.
"A bit of Ysolde's sprig for the goose," she'd say, and pick the grey leaves off the bush by her door.

The winters were the worst on the cart.
We'd be out in sleet and snow, with sacking over our heads and the horse steaming, and I'd think there was no colder place in the world than the top of Brackley hill in January.
Then we'd come down into the village and the carrier's wife would have a fire going and a pot of pottage on it, and I'd think there was no warmer place in the world either.

In the church calendar, basil is set down as Saint Edith's wort.
The curate showed me once, in the big book, with the saint's name in red and a little drawing of the leaves beside it, and he said that's what the old monks called it.

My grandson has finished his page.
He says the curate will want more next week.
I've told him there's always more, if you live long enough.
